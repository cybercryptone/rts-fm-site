Most clubs mark an anniversary with a lineup poster and a door charge. Wilde Renate marked its 19th, this September, with a party that runs 66 hours straight across seven floors. That kind of scale only makes sense once you know what almost didn't happen a year earlier: Berlin's most theatrical DIY club came within months of losing its building entirely, to the same landlord who shut down Watergate for good.

## From Derelict Tenement to DIY Institution

Wilde Renate opened in 2007 inside a genuinely unrenovated apartment building on Alt-Stralau, a quiet peninsula in Friedrichshain across the Spree from Treptower Park. The building had sat mostly derelict since German reunification before a group of Berlin creatives started using it for studio and storage space, a use that gradually tipped over into parties, and then into a club that never bothered to strip out the domestic character of the place it took over. Cracked plaster, mismatched found furniture, and torn wallpaper weren't a design brief. They were just what was already there.

That refusal to renovate is the whole identity. Where a club like Berghain trades on industrial scale and a former power plant's severity, Renate built its reputation on the opposite instinct: rooms that still look like somebody's apartment, if that somebody had wildly eclectic taste and no intention of ever moving out.

## Three Floors, One Vanished Labyrinth

The club runs three differently themed rooms under one roof: the Schwarzer Raum, a black-walled techno room on the ground floor; the Grüner Raum upstairs, given over to house; and the Roter Raum, described by regulars as slow, weird, and trippy disco territory. A large courtyard and garden opens in summer, complete with its own bar and a boat hanging improbably from the trees.

![The courtyard at Wilde Renate, Berlin, with its own bar and garden seating](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/c8/Wilde_Renate_Berlin_Courtyard.jpg/1280px-Wilde_Renate_Berlin_Courtyard.jpg)
*Photo by Tim Gage, CC BY-SA 2.0, via Wikimedia Commons.*

Travel and nightlife writers who've covered the club tend to reach for the same word: theatrical. It gets described less as a venue than as a party labyrinth with living-room flair, a description that holds up once you're actually inside: no two rooms match, hallways double back on themselves, and the grey, unassuming 1980s facade behind the club's high wooden fence gives away almost nothing about what's on the other side of it. That mismatch between the building's exterior and its interior is deliberate, or at least it's been left standing long enough to become part of the point.

For four years, the club's basement held something no longer there to see: Peristal Signum, a scrap-material psychedelic labyrinth built entirely by hand by the artist collective Karmanoia over roughly nine months around 2010. It was the kind of structure that could only exist in a club willing to hand a basement over to artists for the better part of a year with no clear plan for what would come out of it. The installation closed permanently in March 2014, and nothing has replaced it since, but its reputation still shows up in how people describe the club's corridors even now: less a hallway than a passage you navigate rather than simply walk through.

## The Landlord Problem

In August 2024, Renate announced it would close permanently at the end of 2025. The building's landlord, the Padovicz Group, headed by the Berlin property investor Gijora Padovicz, declined to renew the lease and had reportedly doubled the rent in recent years. Renate's own statement pointed to the planned A100 motorway extension as an added pressure on the site, and framed its situation as part of a broader pattern rather than an isolated dispute. It named Watergate specifically, the riverside club that the same landlord had shut down for good only months earlier.

The story could easily have ended there. It didn't. In December 2025, Renate announced it had secured a new ten-year lease, avoiding closure entirely, and apologized for how emotionally charged its original announcement had read. The club went dark briefly after New Year's, then reopened gradually: a soft reopening in late January 2026, followed by a fuller relaunch around Easter with a renovated L-shaped dancefloor and a new Kirsch Audio sound system built around 21-inch bass. A fire had damaged the garden entrance, its ticket booth, fence, and photo booth, at some point during the closure period, though the main building itself came through unaffected.

## Residents and a Growing Family

Renate's current resident roster includes Ady Toledano, Amy Dabbs, Frozilla, and Vio PRG, alongside longer-standing names Johannes Albert, Juan Ramos, and S Ruston. The club also runs its own small releases operation through Bandcamp, including a two-part compilation marking its tenth anniversary in 2017 and later releases such as Yoga At The Dream Haus and Universal Worldwide Weekend, featuring Oskar Szafraniec and Michal Zietara among others.

In 2023, the same team behind Renate opened a second, smaller venue next door on Markgrafendamm: M01, a 400-capacity room with a more experimental, less predictable booking policy than its neighbor. It reads as a pressure valve for the parent club, a space where the operators can take bigger risks without staking the main room's reputation on every night.

## 66 Hours, Seven Floors

This year's anniversary party, running September 11 through 14, was billed as 7 Floors, 66 Hours, spanning both the Renate and M01 buildings. According to DJ Mag's coverage of the announcement, the lineup pairs international names Angel D'lite, Mary Lake, and Daniel Avery with local Berlin talent Handmade, Camilla Rae, Organza, and Madalba, alongside live sets from Schatz, Das Beat, Fritz Ernst, and Dornika. It's a genuinely large undertaking for a club that, a year earlier, wasn't sure it would still have a lease to throw a party on at all.

Renate's own history in polls has been modest by Berlin standards. It placed 94th in DJ Mag's Top 100 Clubs in 2019, its only confirmed appearance in that ranking, and it doesn't chase the metric the way some bigger rooms do. What it has instead is duration: nineteen years in the same unrenovated building, run largely by the same community that started using it for storage before anyone called it a club.

## Berlin's Club Squeeze Isn't Unique to Renate

Renate's standoff with its landlord fits a pattern that's shown up across Europe over the past two years. [Munich's BLITZ closed for good in 2026](/blog/blitz-munich-closure) after nine years as one of Germany's most respected small techno rooms, a closure with no reprieve attached. London's Printworks went the opposite direction, [securing council approval to reopen its Press Halls after a redevelopment fight](/blog/printworks-london-reopening-2026), a rare case of a major closed room actually coming back. And the pressures aren't confined to Germany: [UK nightclubs have been closing at a rate of roughly three a week](/blog/uk-nightclub-closures-licensing-reform), driven by many of the same forces, rising costs, redevelopment pressure, licensing friction, that nearly took Renate down. Even Berlin's most famous room isn't immune to the wider churn: [Berghain itself fell to 21st in DJ Mag's 2026 poll](/blog/berghain-unvrs-dj-mag-top-100), a reminder that no single club's standing is guaranteed, reputation or not. Renate's reprieve is the exception worth paying attention to precisely because so many of these stories don't end that way.

## FAQ

### When did Wilde Renate open?

Wilde Renate opened in 2007 inside a formerly derelict apartment building on Alt-Stralau in Berlin's Friedrichshain district, across the Spree from Treptower Park.

### Is Wilde Renate closing?

No. The club announced a planned closure for the end of 2025 after its landlord, the Padovicz Group, declined to renew its lease, but secured a new ten-year lease in December 2025 and reopened fully by Easter 2026 after a renovation.

### What is the Peristal Signum labyrinth?

Peristal Signum was a scrap-material psychedelic labyrinth built by hand in Wilde Renate's basement by the artist collective Karmanoia, constructed over roughly nine months around 2010. It closed permanently in March 2014.

### Who are Wilde Renate's resident DJs?

Current residents include Ady Toledano, Amy Dabbs, Frozilla, and Vio PRG, alongside longer-standing residents Johannes Albert, Juan Ramos, and S Ruston.

### What is M01?

M01 is a 400-capacity sister venue opened in 2023 by the team behind Wilde Renate, located next door on Markgrafendamm, with a more experimental booking policy than the main club.

Shortly before 8 October 2026, UK culture minister Ian Murray stood in a Brussels music venue and said the UK and the EU are working on "pragmatic joint solutions" to make touring simpler and cheaper. As of 12 October 2026, no deal has been signed. UK artists still get no guaranteed right to work in the EU under the 2020 trade agreement, and the main rules for UK DJs and bands are the same ones that have applied since 1 January 2021: up to 90 days in any 180 without a visa, with paid work governed country by country. For underground electronic music, the practical story is less about big truck convoys and more about permits, tax paperwork, merch and vinyl shipping, and the cost of saying yes to a small booking.

This article is not legal, tax or immigration advice. Rules differ by country, depend on your nationality and the kind of work you do, and change often. Before you travel or book, check the official guidance of each country you will work in, and ask a specialist adviser if the stakes are high.

## What did the UK minister actually say about touring?

Ian Murray, Minister of State at the Department for Digital, Culture, Media and Sport, said the UK government wants "real progress" on touring in Europe and that fans are "losing the chance to experience music from across Europe." That wording is the basis for [MusicRadar's report of 9 October 2026](https://www.musicradar.com/music-industry/fans-are-losing-the-chance-to-experience-music-from-across-europe-and-critically-opportunities-for-us-all-to-tell-each-others-stories-through-music-uk-minister-bemoans-barriers-to-music-industry-put-up-by-brexit), written by Beth Simpson.

The fullest account of the speech we found is [NME's report of 8 October 2026](https://www.nme.com/news/music/brexit-music-touring-crisis-update-brussels-european-interest-progress-3972626), by Andrew Trendell. It says Murray spoke at a reception at the Ancienne Belgique in Brussels, after meeting senior EU representatives, European legislators and music industry stakeholders. NME dates the event only as "last week" and we could not find a gov.uk transcript, so we have not given a precise date.

According to NME, Murray framed the issue as a "shared European interest" rather than a UK-only complaint and said both sides were working to "explore pragmatic joint solutions" to make touring "simpler and more affordable, particularly for emerging artists." He cited government-commissioned research putting the cost of reduced UK touring in the EU at £208 million in lost economic value and 2,500 jobs for the UK, and around £1 billion and more than 17,000 jobs across the EU. MusicRadar adds a cautious note: EU negotiations are slow, and it expects the carnet, the customs document for touring equipment, to remain a fact of life for some time.

Murray was not announcing a new policy. He did not, in the coverage we read, announce a date for any agreement or a specific legal change. MusicRadar also reports that Prime Minister Andy Burnham told the Labour conference the week before that he would "look at the options" on the UK's relationship with the EU, including, it says, rejoining. That is wider EU politics rather than a touring measure, and the touring coverage does not tie the two together.

### Where do the £208 million and 17,000 jobs come from?

They come from [The economic benefits of touring and impact of EU exit](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/the-economic-benefits-of-touring-and-impact-of-eu-exit/the-economic-benefits-of-touring-and-impact-of-eu-exit), a report by Ipsos and Nordicity commissioned by DCMS and published on 27 August 2026. It estimates that between 2022 and 2024 the UK lost £208.0 million in gross value added and 2,490 full-time equivalent jobs across the touring sectors covered, which include live music, orchestras, theatre, dance and visual arts. For EU host countries it estimates about £1.04 billion in gross value added and 17,010 full-time equivalent jobs, with Germany hit hardest at nearly £290 million and about 4,000 jobs.

Three caveats matter. First, the report itself says its findings are "indicative and correlational," and its model compares UK touring in the EU with a US counterfactual. Second, the interview strand is small: 13 in-depth interviews, which the authors say should be treated with caution. Third, the report does not break out DJs, and its core data is PRS for Music records of performances reported by members, so DJ-led careers are probably only partly visible in it. That last point is our reading, not the authors' statement.

## What changed for touring after Brexit?

Since 1 January 2021, UK performers have been third-country nationals in the EU. The Trade and Cooperation Agreement (TCA), agreed in December 2020, has no dedicated provisions for touring artists, so a UK DJ needs to meet each member state's own rules for paid work, while UK-registered vehicles and touring equipment face haulage and customs rules that did not exist before.

### What is the 90 days in any 180 rule?

UK passport holders can enter the Schengen area for up to 90 days in any 180-day period without a visa, but the DCMS report stresses this "does not include an automatic right to undertake paid work." Paid-work rules vary by member state, covering who counts as an artist, how long you can work, what paperwork you need and whether you must show proof of income or savings. Ireland is the main exception to the pattern, because the Common Travel Area still lets UK and Irish musicians play in each other's countries without restriction, as [Free Movement's 2021 explainer](https://freemovement.org.uk/the-row-over-post-brexit-visas-for-musicians-explained/) notes.

How many EU countries let UK performers work without a permit is a moving number, so treat any figure with its date. In August 2021 the UK government said 19 member states had confirmed UK performers did not need visas or work permits for short tours, and it had approached Spain, Croatia, Greece, Portugal, Bulgaria, Romania, Malta and Cyprus about the rest, per [NME's report of 4 August 2021](https://www.nme.com/news/music/visa-free-touring-granted-for-uk-artists-in-19-eu-countries-3010866). In September 2024, [UK in a Changing Europe](https://ukandeu.ac.uk/explainers/easing-restrictions-on-touring-artists/) said 24 of 27 member states offered some visa and work permit free route for touring artists and support staff, with durations from a single performance up to 90 days. We could not find a current country-by-country list from an official source, so you need to check each country directly.

### What about carnets, instruments and trucks?

Equipment is the other big shift. The DCMS report says touring productions now rely on ATA carnets or temporary admission procedures. It puts the UK cost of a carnet at roughly £270 to £444 including VAT, plus a security deposit of 30 to 40 percent of the value of the goods listed, and notes penalties in some cases reportedly reaching up to 40 percent of the declared value. Instruments containing protected materials can also need CITES documentation, which matters mostly for older acoustic instruments rather than synthesisers and controllers.

Haulage is governed by cabotage rules, which limit how a foreign-registered vehicle can load and unload within a country. The DCMS report's table says that before the TCA, cross-trade movements were unlimited and cabotage allowed three movements in seven days. After the TCA, a UK vehicle can make two cross-trade movements, or one cross-trade and one cabotage movement. The UK introduced a dual registration measure in 2022 to help specialist events hauliers, but DCMS notes not all operators qualify.

### What about merch, tax and social security?

Merchandise sold on tour is now treated as a commercial import into the EU. The report says import VAT and sometimes duties are due at first entry, businesses may need an EORI number, consignments above £2,500 generally need an export declaration leaving the UK, and commercial goods above €1,000 generally need declaring entering the EU. Many costs can be reclaimed, but reimbursement can be slow and unpredictable. Some acts respond by holding EU-based stock, which cuts margins.

Withholding tax on performance fees and A1 social security certificates add cash-flow pressure. An A1 confirms which country's social security system a worker pays into while working abroad temporarily. The report says delays in obtaining A1 forms have in some cases led European promoters to withhold part of fees.

| Issue | Before 2021 | Since 1 January 2021 | Who feels it most |
|---|---|---|---|
| Entering to work | Free movement, no permits | Up to 90 days in any 180 visa-free, paid work set by each member state | DJs on multi-country runs, residencies |
| Short-notice gigs | Add a date or festival slot freely | More paperwork and risk; DCMS says last-minute slots have reduced | Emerging and mid-level DJs |
| Equipment | No customs paperwork | Carnet or temporary admission, plus CITES for some instruments | Live acts, bands, large rigs; DJs less so |
| Trucks and vans | Unlimited cross-trade, three cabotage movements in seven days | Two cross-trade movements, or one plus one cabotage | Tours with their own vehicles |
| Merch and records | Moved within the single market | Import VAT, customs declarations, EORI, possible duty | Labels, small acts selling merch |
| Fees and tax | Single market rules | Withholding tax, reclaim delays, A1 certificates | Anyone paid by EU promoters |

## Where do UK-EU touring talks stand in 2026?

As of 12 October 2026, we found no agreed visa waiver, carnet exemption or cabotage change for touring artists. What exists is a political commitment, a lobbying coalition, new evidence and a government response that is cautious on unilateral action.

- **19 May 2025:** The first UK-EU summit produced a "Common Understanding" that says the European Commission and the UK "recognise the value of travel and cultural and artistic exchanges, including the activities of touring artists," and will continue efforts to support them. The wording is quoted in the DCMS report.
- **December 2025:** The Cultural Exchange Coalition was established, co-chaired by Anita Debaere of Pearle Live Performance Europe and Dave Webster of the Musicians' Union, according to [UK Music](https://www.ukmusic.org/policy-campaigns/touring-and-working-in-the-eu/).
- **24 March 2026:** At the coalition's launch in Brussels, Murray promised a "full commitment" and said there is "a real political will" on both sides, per [UK Music](https://www.ukmusic.org/news/uk-minister-ian-murray-pledges-full-commitment-of-uk-government-to-tear-down-barriers-to-touring-eu-and-uk/). UK Music chief executive Tom Kiehl said "the current deal is not the best that we can do."
- **15 June 2026:** The Commons Culture, Media and Sport Committee published its report. As [NME reported](https://www.nme.com/news/music/mps-share-report-and-call-on-government-to-finally-break-down-brexit-barriers-of-artists-touring-europe-3951040), it urged the government to work with the EU and member states, re-engage with Creative Europe and its proposed successor AgoraEU, and negotiate on customs, short-term touring and haulage. Chair Caroline Dinenage said the aim was not "renegotiating Brexit" but addressing issues "that weren't covered off in the first place."
- **22 June 2026:** Keir Starmer announced his resignation, and the second UK-EU summit planned for 22 July was postponed. European Commission spokesperson Paula Pinho said "Postponed is not cancelled," according to [Brussels Signal](https://brusselssignal.eu/2026/06/eu-and-uk-postpone-post-brexit-summit-after-starmer-quits/). That report lists emissions trading, agri-food checks, energy and youth mobility as the agenda and does not mention touring.
- **7 September 2026:** [Complete Music Update](https://completemusicupdate.com/no-tangible-progress-on-brexit-touring-barriers-say-alarmed-mps/) reported the government's response to the committee. Ministers agree barriers persist and want "tangible progress," but they have no current plans to subsidise carnets or offer a carnet guarantee, and will improve existing guidance rather than create a touring advice hub. Dinenage said the committee was "alarmed" by the lack of progress. No new summit date was given.

On substance, the industry's asks are consistent. UK Music wants a bespoke visa waiver agreement for cultural touring, a longer-term touring visa, cultural exemptions on carnets and cabotage, simpler merch rules and rejoining Creative Europe. The European Commission's public position, as summarised by UK in a Changing Europe in 2024, was that it would not rewrite TCA rules on services, customs or haulage. UK Music's current page says a new EU Visa Strategy commits to exploring "new rules for extended short stays." We could not confirm what, if anything, that means for UK performers.

Both sides have an argument. The UK government's line in 2021, quoted by Attack Magazine, was that the EU "did not offer a deal that works for musicians." A broader argument for the current settlement, which we are characterising rather than quoting, is that any performer exemption has to be reciprocal and so touches wider questions about movement of people. Campaigners reply that touring is a narrow, mutual interest, and point to the DCMS report's estimate that EU host countries lost about £1.04 billion against £208 million for the UK, although the two figures cover different scopes. The 2020 negotiation itself remains disputed: the UK government said the EU rejected its proposals, while EU sources said the offer came from them and the UK turned it down, as [Free Movement](https://freemovement.org.uk/the-row-over-post-brexit-visas-for-musicians-explained/) reported at the time without ruling on who was right.

## What does it mean for underground DJs, labels and festivals?

For club DJs the frictions are real but uneven. Heavy kit matters less, short weekend trips matter more, and paperwork and tax costs bite hardest on small fees. No source we found measures DJ-only outcomes, so what follows mixes surveys of musicians generally with DJ-specific commentary and our own reasoning.

### How are UK DJs affected in EU clubs and festivals?

The strongest quantified evidence covers musicians in general. The Musicians' Union's April and May 2024 survey found that among those who had worked in the EU before Brexit, 75 percent had fewer bookings, 79 percent could not make up the lost work elsewhere, and 59 percent said EU touring was no longer financially viable, as cited in the DCMS report. UK Music's 2023 survey of 1,461 creators found 30 percent said Brexit had affected their earnings, and 82 percent of those said income fell. Neither survey is DJ-only.

On DJs specifically, early commentary was more measured than the headline numbers. [Mixmag's February 2021 feature](https://mixmag.net/feature/brexit-touring-djs-electronic-musicians-artists-europe-eu) quoted immigration expert Steve Richard saying most EU countries do not require visa-exempt nationals to get work permits for a few days' work, and said DJs who travel light are unlikely to need carnets. [Attack Magazine's January 2021 piece](https://www.attackmagazine.com/features/long-read/brexit-what-next-for-uk-djs-performers/) reported an open letter from artists including Simian Mobile Disco, Ross From Friends, Jayda G and Bicep warning that "The extra costs will make many tours unviable." Both can be true: a single weekend in Germany is manageable, while a run of club dates across several countries is where the count of days, the permits and the tax admin add up.

The DCMS report adds detail relevant to club-level bookings. UK artists' performances at smaller general live music venues in Germany fell from about 5,000 in 2019 to about 3,600 in 2024 in PRS data, and in Spain from about 2,100 to about 1,400, while Italy's fell by only around 2 percent. That spread supports the point that the rules and enforcement differ by country. It also says the shift has been toward shorter, segmented tours and fewer last-minute festival additions, and [NME reported on 31 March 2026](https://www.nme.com/news/music/brexit-touring-2026-update-uk-europe-progress-action-visas-costs-3937694) that crew were sent home from Adele's German residency because of the 90 in 180 rule. That is not a DJ example, but it shows why extended residencies are where day-counting gets serious.

Our own festival pages show where UK DJs are most likely to cross borders. Germany and the Netherlands, which were both among the 19 confirmed in 2021, host events including [Time Warp](/festivals/time-warp), [Dekmantel](/festivals/dekmantel-festival) and [Awakenings](/festivals/awakenings-festival), and each has its own country hub for [Germany](/festivals/country/germany) and the [Netherlands](/festivals/country/netherlands). [Sónar](/festivals/sonar) in Spain, [Dimensions](/festivals/dimensions-festival) in Croatia, [Waking Life](/festivals/waking-life) in Portugal and [Sunwaves](/festivals/sunwaves) in Bulgaria are in countries that were on the 2021 list of states not yet confirmed. We have not verified their current positions, so check the relevant official guidance before assuming either way.

### How are EU DJs affected when they play the UK?

EU nationals can visit the UK without a visa, though most European visitors now need an electronic travel authorisation, which [gov.uk lists at £20](https://www.gov.uk/electronic-travel-authorisation). Paid work is the issue. [Gov.uk's guidance for creative professionals from non-visa countries](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/visiting-the-uk-as-a-creative-professional-from-a-non-visa-national-country) sets out the main routes: a permitted paid engagement of up to 30 days with a formal invitation from a UK organisation; a creative worker concession of up to three months with a certificate of sponsorship; a permit-free festival route of up to six months; and a full creative worker visa for up to 12 months.

The permit-free festival route is the one that matters for festivals. The [Home Office list](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/immigration-rules/immigration-rules-appendix-visitor-permit-free-festival-list), updated on 8 October 2026, names 72 festivals including Creamfields, Parklife, Wireless Festival, The Warehouse Project and Boomtown. We did not find Houghton or Junction 2 on it. Under the [application guidance](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/apply-to-be-on-the-list-of-permit-free-festivals/how-to-apply-to-get-on-the-list-of-permit-free-festivals), new entrants need at least three years of history, audiences of at least 15,000 at each of the last three editions and at least 15 non-British or Irish performers at each. Smaller independent events such as [Houghton](/festivals/houghton-festival) and [Junction 2](/festivals/junction-2) may therefore rely on other routes. We have not confirmed which they use, and promoters should check with the Home Office.

NME's March 2026 report also says EU artists touring the UK have reportedly been refused entry, and that one was detained. We treat those as reported claims, not verified incidents.

### What about labels, vinyl and merch?

The DCMS research covers touring merchandise, not record distribution, so it does not tell us about label shipping. What it does show is the pattern: import VAT at first entry, divergent national rules and a shift to EU-based fulfilment. For UK labels sending vinyl to EU customers the same logic applies, and a new layer arrived on 1 July 2026. [Velveteen reported](https://www.velveteen.fm/news/bandcamp-eu-small-parcel-customs-duty), citing Bandcamp, that physical goods under €150 entering the EU from outside the bloc can face a temporary customs duty of €3 per item category, running until 1 July 2028. The page does not mention the UK, so UK sellers should check how it applies to them.

This sits on top of an already tight vinyl supply chain, which we covered in [our report on pressing plant capacity](/blog/vinyl-pressing-plant-capacity-crisis). For a small label, the practical question is no longer only how many records to press, but where to hold stock.

### How does this connect to the wider club economy?

Touring friction lands on an already stretched scene. Our [analysis of DJ economics](/blog/underground-dj-economics-data) found that only 1.6 percent of Resident Advisor's DJ profiles had five or more upcoming bookings, and that, in GigXchange's 2026 report, typical UK musician income is £20,700 with 43 percent of musicians earning under £14,000 (the report has no DJ-specific fee band). When incomes are that thin, a fixed cost such as a carnet, an accountant's time on withholding tax or a missed day on the 90 in 180 count is a large share of the night. That connects to the venue side too, covered in our reports on [why the UK keeps losing its nightclubs](/blog/uk-nightclub-closures-licensing-reform), [Europe's club closures](/blog/europe-club-closures-tracker) and [European festival cancellations](/blog/europe-festival-cancellations-2026).

## What we make of it

This section is RTS.FM's own opinion and analysis, not reported fact. Each point comes with the strongest counter-argument we can think of.

**1. The official numbers probably miss most of our scene.** The DCMS model rests on PRS performance data and a small set of interviews, and does not mention DJs once. Underground DJs, small labels and promoters sit at the thin end of the economy the report describes, where fees are low and costs are fixed. So we would not read £208 million as a DJ number. *The strongest counter-argument: DJs travel light, play short trips and rarely need carnets or trucks, so they may be among the least affected performers, and the report's focus on bands may be correct.*

**2. The fix club DJs need is simple: a clear permit rule and easy tax paperwork.** For a DJ on short bookings, the most useful outcome would be a plain visa and work permit exemption for short engagements, plus a faster A1 and withholding tax process. Carnet reform matters most to bands and labels. *The strongest counter-argument: a cultural exemption is exactly what the Commission has resisted since 2020, it may be hard to limit to musicians, and a narrow deal that leaves carnets and A1 delays untouched could disappoint everyone.*

**3. Large festivals can absorb these costs, and small ones cannot.** Events on the Home Office's permit-free list have an easier way to book EU artists. A 15,000 audience threshold favours the biggest operators. Small independent festivals and club nights, which carry much of the underground's identity, face more admin per artist. We think that tilts bookings toward bigger names and bigger events. *The strongest counter-argument: other routes exist, such as the 30 day permitted paid engagement, and some promoters may find these routes straightforward in practice, so the effect on lineups could be smaller than we assume.*

**4. Plan for no change, and treat any deal as a bonus.** The talks have political momentum, but the summit was postponed, the government has ruled out carnet subsidies for now, and the Commission was unwilling to reopen TCA services rules as of 2024. If you book or play across the Channel, build costs and day counts around the current rules. *The strongest counter-argument: the mood has shifted with a new Prime Minister, a minister in Brussels, a government report and an organised coalition, so a deal on mobility may come faster than the record suggests, and planning too conservatively could mean turning down gigs you could have taken.*

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Which minister made the comments about fans losing music from across Europe?

Ian Murray, Minister of State at the Department for Digital, Culture, Media and Sport. NME reported on 8 October 2026 that he spoke at the Ancienne Belgique in Brussels after meeting EU representatives, European legislators and music industry stakeholders, and MusicRadar reported the remarks on 9 October 2026.

### Is there a visa-free touring deal between the UK and EU in 2026?

Not in anything we could find as of 12 October 2026. The 2020 trade agreement has no dedicated touring provisions, the May 2025 summit produced only a commitment to support cultural exchange, and the second summit planned for 22 July 2026 was postponed. Ministers describe talks on solutions but have not announced an agreement.

### How long can a UK DJ stay in the EU without a visa?

UK passport holders can stay up to 90 days in any 180-day period in the Schengen area without a visa. That limit counts all days across all Schengen countries, and it is a limit on visits, not a right to work. Ireland is outside Schengen and has separate arrangements with the UK.

### Do UK DJs need a work permit to play a one-off gig in the EU?

It depends on the country. Some member states allow short paid performances without a permit, and others require a visa or permit or ask for supporting documents. The number of countries with such exemptions has been reported as 19 in 2021 and 24 of 27 in 2024, so check the country's official guidance and ask your promoter or agent for written confirmation.

### Do DJs need a carnet for their equipment?

Often not, if you carry only a laptop, USB drives and headphones, although this is a customs question and rules vary. Carnets matter more for controllers, mixers, synthesisers, drum machines and other bulky or valuable kit. The DCMS report puts a UK carnet at roughly £270 to £444 including VAT plus a security deposit of 30 to 40 percent of the goods' value.

### What is cabotage and does it affect DJs?

Cabotage rules limit how a foreign-registered vehicle can load and unload goods inside another country. After the 2020 trade agreement, UK vehicles can make fewer such movements in the EU. It mainly affects tours that run their own trucks or vans, so most solo DJs are not directly affected, though it can raise costs for larger shows and festivals.

### What is an A1 certificate?

It is a document that confirms which country's social security system a worker pays into while working temporarily abroad. The DCMS report says delays in getting A1 certificates have in some cases led European promoters to withhold part of a fee. Whether you need one depends on your status and the country, so check with the relevant authorities.

### Can EU DJs play UK clubs and festivals freely?

They can visit the UK without a visa but usually need an electronic travel authorisation, and paid work is limited. Routes include a permitted paid engagement of up to 30 days with a formal UK invitation, a creative worker concession with a certificate of sponsorship, and a permit-free festival route of up to six months for events on the Home Office list.

### How does Brexit affect small labels selling vinyl and merch in the EU?

UK shipments to the EU are treated as imports, so customs declarations, import VAT and sometimes an EORI number can apply. The DCMS report found many touring acts moving to EU-based stock to cut margin loss. Bandcamp has also said a temporary EU customs duty of 3 euros per item category applies to some physical orders under 150 euros from outside the EU since 1 July 2026.

### Will anything change for touring soon?

Nobody can say. Ministers say they are working on joint solutions, the Cultural Exchange Coalition is lobbying, and a parliamentary committee has called for action. But the government's September 2026 response ruled out carnet subsidies for now, and no date for a new UK-EU summit was announced in the reports we read. Plan around current rules.

Somewhere in Los Angeles, a Disney-owned studio is writing jokes about the most famous "no" in club culture. Searchlight Entertainment, the specialty label behind awards-season fare like *Poor Things* and *The Banshees of Inisherin*, is developing an untitled comedy film about two Americans who fly to Berlin and fail to get past the bouncers at Berghain, according to [Resident Advisor](https://ra.co/news/84164) and [NME](https://www.nme.com/news/music/a-new-comedy-film-based-on-trying-to-get-into-berghain-is-in-the-works-3920202). It is, on paper, an odd pairing: a Mouse House studio and the club that has spent two decades refusing to explain itself to anyone, including, apparently, Hollywood.

## What Is the New Berghain Movie Actually About?

The project is co-written by and will star Bowen Yang and Matt Rogers, the *Saturday Night Live* alum and the stand-up comic and podcaster, respectively, reuniting after their 2022 queer rom-com *Fire Island*. According to [Deadline's original report](https://deadline.com/2025/12/bowen-yang-matt-rogers-developing-searchlight-comedy-1236635972/), Olivia Gerke and Oly Obst are producing through 3 Arts Entertainment, and the film pulls its premise directly from a real, documented trip: two Americans who crossed the Atlantic specifically to get into Berghain, and got turned away.

As of this writing, the film has no title, no director attached, and no release date. Searchlight confirmed the project in early December 2025, and public reporting has not moved much past that initial announcement through the middle of 2026, which tracks for a comedy still in the writing stage rather than production.

## Who Are Bowen Yang and Matt Rogers, and Why Give Them This Story?

Yang and Rogers have spent a decade building a specific comic voice together on *Las Culturistas*, the pop-culture podcast they have co-hosted since 2016, which won iHeartRadio's Podcast of the Year in both 2023 and 2025. Yang is a five-time Emmy nominee for his work on *SNL* and also appeared in *Wicked*; Rogers has credits on *Search Party*, *Shrill*, and *I Love That For You*, alongside a stand-up career built on exactly the kind of hyper-specific, insider-baseball cultural commentary that a Berghain door story demands. Casting two proudly online, extremely online-coded comedians as the ones who don't make the cut is, itself, sort of the joke.

## What Actually Happened in the Search Engine Podcast Episode?

The film's source material is a two-part episode of the podcast *Search Engine*, hosted by PJ Vogt, titled "[Why didn't Chris and Dan get into Berghain?](https://www.searchengine.show/why-didnt-chris-and-dan-get-into-berghain-part-2/)" and released in June 2024. It follows two friends, Chris and Dan, who fly from the United States to Berlin specifically to get into the club, get rejected at the door, and then spend the rest of the episode trying to reverse-engineer why. Vogt and his *Search Engine* co-creator Sruthi Pinnamaneni are both attached as co-producers on the film adaptation, per Deadline, meaning the people who originally reported the rejection are now helping fictionalize it.

It is worth sitting with why that premise works as a movie at all. A story about failing to get into a club is, structurally, a story about wanting something you can't fully explain and being denied it by someone who won't explain either. That is comedy gold, but it's also a pretty accurate description of how Berghain actually operates.

## Why Is Berghain's Door So Notoriously Hard to Get Through?

Because nobody, including the club, will say what the actual rules are. Berghain, which opened in a former East Berlin power plant on October 15, 2004, has no guest list, no bottle service, no VIP line, and a strict no-photography policy once you're inside, all of which is well documented on the club's own [Wikipedia entry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berghain) and in years of nightlife journalism. The wait to even reach the door can run for hours, and the vast majority of people who make it that far are turned away without a reason given.

### Sven Marquardt, the Face at the Front of the Line

The bouncer most associated with that door is Sven Marquardt, a photographer from the former East Berlin who has run Berghain's door team since the club opened. Marquardt has said publicly that his decisions are "subjective," built on instinct rather than a checklist, and that the point of the filtering isn't snobbery for its own sake but protecting what he's described as the club's function as a safe space, one that depends on keeping out anyone who might disrupt it, whether that's stag parties, phone-wielding tourists, or people who simply seem like they're there for the wrong reasons. A recent academic study out of Berlin, [covered by Euronews](https://euronews.com/culture/2025/03/26/berghain-more-berg-nein-study-probes-the-logic-behind-berlin-club-door-policies), even tried to formally probe the logic behind Berlin's club door policies, underlining just how much cultural fascination the mystery still generates a full generation after Berghain opened.

### The Part Most People Get Wrong

The door isn't random cruelty, and it isn't really about fashion, either, despite the meme version of Berghain that circulates online (all black clothing, stern faces, vague threats). Longtime Berlin clubbers generally describe it as an attempt to read whether someone understands and respects the space they're trying to enter, in a venue built explicitly around underground, LGBTQ+, and fetish club history rather than as a tourist attraction that happens to play techno. Tourists absolutely do get in. Locals absolutely get rejected. That inconsistency is precisely what makes it maddening, and precisely what makes it a workable premise for a two-hander comedy.

## Why Does This Matter Beyond a Meme, Inside Techno Culture Itself?

Because Berghain isn't just a hard door, it's arguably the single most consequential room in the history of modern techno, and that status has been recognized well beyond dance music press. In 2016, a German court ruled that Berghain produces work of genuine cultural significance, entitling the club to the same reduced tax rate as museums and concert halls rather than being taxed as ordinary entertainment. In 2024, Berlin's broader club and techno culture was added to Germany's national inventory of UNESCO intangible cultural heritage, a recognition [reported by outlets including Designboom](https://www.designboom.com/art/berlin-techno-unesco-world-heritage-status-03-16-2024/), placing all-night dancing in the same bureaucratic category as centuries-old folk traditions.

None of that has made Berghain invincible, either commercially or reputationally. It's still Berlin's only club in the world's most-watched club ranking, but even it isn't immune to shifting industry gravity, as we covered when [Berghain Falls to 21st as a Brand-New Ibiza Hyperclub Takes DJ Mag's No. 1 Spot](/blog/berghain-unvrs-dj-mag-top-100). A Hollywood comedy arriving right as Berghain's numeric ranking slides is its own strange bit of timing, proof that cultural weight and chart position don't move in lockstep.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the new Berghain movie actually called?

It doesn't have a title yet. Searchlight, Bowen Yang, and Matt Rogers confirmed the project in December 2025 as an untitled comedy, and no director, cast beyond Yang and Rogers, or release date has been announced as of this writing.

### Can tourists actually get into Berghain?

Yes, regularly, but there's no formula. The door is run on instinct by a small team led by Sven Marquardt, who has said the process is subjective rather than rule-based, which is exactly why so many visitors, including the two subjects of the podcast this film is based on, fly across an ocean and still get turned away.

### Is the film based on a true story?

Yes. It's adapted from a real reported trip documented in *Search Engine*'s two-part episode "Why didn't Chris and Dan get into Berghain?", in which two Americans travel to Berlin specifically to get into the club and are rejected at the door, then try to figure out why.

### Why is Berghain considered so important to techno culture specifically?

Beyond the door mythology, Berghain has functioned since 2004 as a hub connecting Berlin's underground, LGBTQ+, and industrial-techno lineage to the wider dance music world, a significance formal enough that German courts and UNESCO's national heritage process have both recognized it. It remains the only Berlin club in DJ Mag's global Top 100, even as its exact rank has shifted.

### Is Berghain's door policy actually written down anywhere?

No. The club has never published formal criteria, and Marquardt himself has described the process as subjective judgment rather than a checklist, which is part of why an entire podcast episode, and now a movie, exists just to try to explain a single "no."

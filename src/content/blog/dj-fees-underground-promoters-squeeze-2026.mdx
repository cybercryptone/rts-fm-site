Partly supported. The claim that DJ fees are unreasonable has real evidence behind it, but the evidence is thinner and more concentrated than the argument usually suggests. In a 2022 survey by the UK's Night Time Industries Association (NTIA), 61.5% of 325 nightlife operators said they were paying more for artists and DJs than before the pandemic, according to [Vice's report](https://www.vice.com/en/article/is-clubbing-more-expensive-now). In 2025, [70% of 245 German venues in the LiveKomm monitor](https://miz.org/en/news/club-und-festival-monitoring-der-livekomm-zeigt-schwierige-finanzielle-lage-kleiner-clubs-und-festivals) reported rising artist fee demands. And in France, [107 festivals aided by the national music centre (CNM)](https://cnm.fr/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/CNM_Economie-des-festivals-2024_Publication.pdf) spent 9% more on buying shows in 2024 than in 2023 while programming 2% more acts. Against that, the same festivals' technical costs are a larger share of the bill, no public index measures what a DJ is actually paid, and [43% of UK musicians earn under £14,000 a year from music](https://www.helpmusicians.org.uk/media-and-press-office/first-ever-musicians-census-provides-unparalleled-insight-into-of-the-careers-of-the-uks-music-creators). Our verdict is that fee inflation is concentrated at the top of the market and felt downstream by promoters, small clubs, residents and audiences.

## Are DJ fees really out of control?

At the very top of the market, probably yes, but nobody publishes a fee index, so no one can prove it. What exists is three kinds of indirect evidence: operator surveys, festival accounts, and a complaint that has been repeated, almost word for word, for thirty years.

Start with the repetition, because it is the most useful corrective to the idea that this is a 2026 crisis. In November 1996, [Mixmag ran a piece, reposted by 909originals in 2021](https://909originals.com/2021/01/28/money-money-money-has-clubland-gone-too-commercial-november-1996/), arguing that top DJs' fees had been rising at a ludicrous rate over the previous 18 months. It listed standard fees of £1,500 for Sasha and £1,000 for Carl Cox, then agents' New Year's Eve offers of £10,000 and £8,000 for the same two. In January 2015, ID&T founder Duncan Stutterheim told a Dutch conference, as [reported by Event Planner](https://eventplanner.net/news/6755_expensive-djs-are-killing-the-festival-business.html) and [Dancing Astronaut](https://dancingastronaut.com/2015/01/idt-founder-duncan-stutterheim-discusses-sky-high-booking-fees-djs-ask-much-money-killing-business/), that big DJs had raised prices roughly tenfold, that fees of €40,000 or more made many festivals unprofitable, and that the most popular 10% of DJs take about 90% of the money. Mixmag's [November 2019 feature on soaring DJ fees](https://mixmag.net/feature/unhealthy-scene-how-sky-high-dj-fees-are-crippling-dance-music) warned that the clubbing bubble could burst. [Resident Advisor in June 2023](https://ra.co/news/78323) said a post-lockdown boom had pushed booking fees up. [Dazed in August 2026](https://www.dazeddigital.com/music/article/70763/1/hot-take-its-time-we-paid-dj-less-money-fred-again-music-venues-clubs) ran an opinion column arguing that the biggest DJs should be paid less.

Repetition cuts both ways. It means the complaint is not new, and that clubs, festivals and promoters have survived many such cycles. It also means the structural problem, a small number of acts who can reliably sell tickets and a large number of promoters competing for them, has never been fixed.

Two points from that table matter for the rest of this piece. First, almost every number is either old, anecdotal, or a survey of how operators feel. Second, the best recent figures, from France, show fees rising faster than inflation and faster than the number of acts booked, but they come from festivals, are not specific to DJs, and sit beside a technical-services line that rose 30% in the same year.

We also tried to cross-check the GigXchange report that our [earlier piece on DJ economics](/blog/underground-dj-economics-data) used for a typical UK evening DJ slot. The text we could extract from [GigXchange's 2026 PDF](https://gigxchange.app/rates/reports/musician-earnings-2026.pdf) gives UK musician averages but does not show a DJ-specific fee band, so we have not relied on that band here and have corrected our earlier piece accordingly. And the [Attack Magazine analysis of Resident Advisor's database](https://www.attackmagazine.com/features/long-read/the-1-reality-what-the-numbers-tell-us-about-dj-economics/) behind the 1.6% figure counts DJs with five or more upcoming bookings; it contains no fee data, so it measures how many DJs are working, not what they are paid.

Three things stand out. The complaints come from three different kinds of business: festival organisers (Stutterheim), mid-sized and small club promoters (Sumner, O'Gorman, Howe), and large operators who set the market (Blackett at fabric). The people at the top of that list are not outsiders. Blackett's own line in the 2019 piece was that "We've all been complicit in this", and he went on to describe fee structures fabric had changed, which is more useful than blame.

Second, the voices from outside the UK and Germany, in the 2023 Resident Advisor piece, add a dimension that the British debate misses. A promoter in Prague or Bogotá pays international DJs in dollars or euros, so currency moves and flight prices can matter more than the headline fee itself.

Third, the sourcing is uneven. The most-quoted line in this debate, the 80% figure, is one programmer's description of a pattern and one line of the author's narration. It travels well in summaries, and [our own nightlife data piece](/blog/why-people-go-out-less-nightlife-data-2026) carried it with a warning that it is dated and anecdotal. It is not a measured average, and Mixmag did not present it as one.

The agents' side is thinner, because agents rarely go on record. The 2019 feature includes two anonymous agents: one said high fees usually reflect the crowds and revenue a DJ brings, the other said artists can switch agents if they feel underserved. A UTA agent in the 2023 RA piece said fees should be decided case by case with promoters providing evidence.

## Where does the money go in a club night?

In the few budgets that have been described publicly, the headline fee is the largest controllable cost, but staff, energy, rent and bar supply costs have risen too, and in Berlin the revenue mix has flipped so that the door now matters more than the bar. That makes a fee mistake more expensive than it used to be.

The 80% figure is worth unpacking, because it is the number most often used to prove the thesis. In the 2019 Mixmag feature, Blackett described promoters paying about 80% of a night's budget to the headliner and then booking local DJs; the article's own narration says a single top-tier DJ can account for 80% of a club's budget. The sentence that follows in Mixmag is the useful one: that money has to come from somewhere. It comes from the rest of the line-up, from marketing, and from the promoter's margin. What the piece does not provide is an average across clubs or a sample.

Other numbers give the surrounding structure.

- **Door price held flat while costs rose.** [Vice reported in August 2022](https://www.vice.com/en/article/is-clubbing-more-expensive-now) that fabric's average weekend door price was £18.95 against £19.16 in 2019, while Blackett said bar supply prices had roughly tripled since 2019. In the same piece, the NTIA found that more than half of respondents reported a 30% rise in operating costs.
- **A flipped revenue mix in Berlin.** The [Clubcommission's 2026 study](https://www.clubcommission.de/neue-studie-zur-berliner-clubkultur/), summarised by [Pollstar](https://news.pollstar.com/2026/08/10/business-model-flipped-on-its-head-findings-from-berlin-club-study/), found door income at 59% of revenue against 21% in 2017, and food and drink at about 20% against 60%. 61% of the clubs covered their costs, against 79% in 2017. The study had 102 completed responses.
- **Where the Berlin clubs say the pressure is.** Staff costs (64%), overheads (62%), falling guest spending power (60%) and rent (54%) were the top pressures named. The summaries we read do not list artist fees at all, which is a reminder that fee inflation is one pressure among several and may not be the main one for every room.
- **What a night out costs the guest.** Hegemann told Mixmag in February 2025 that a typical night out for a young clubber in Germany runs to about €80 once entry, drinks and a taxi are counted, which he said pushed many to go out about once every six weeks rather than weekly.

So what does this mean for the fee? When a club depends on the door for nearly 60% of revenue, as the Berlin sample now does, a headliner who fails to move tickets leaves almost nothing to fall back on, because the bar can no longer subsidise a miss. That is the mechanism behind the promoters' complaint. A fee that is reasonable for a DJ who reliably sells 1,000 tickets is ruinous for one who sells 300. Hegemann's own account is exactly this: an act that fills Tresor in Berlin, he said, may draw only about 200 people in Dortmund.

fabric's response, as described by Mixmag in 2019, is the clearest worked example of a promoter trying to share that risk. Where an artist's flat fee would have been £7,000, fabric offered £4,000 as a base, rising to £10,000 if the night sold out. If nobody turned up, the club saved £3,000 and the artist still received £4,000. The artist loses certainty and gains upside; the club caps its downside. We return to this in the section on models.

For the wider picture of why independent clubs are failing even where audiences are not, see our [piece on why people go out less](/blog/why-people-go-out-less-nightlife-data-2026), our [UK closures and licensing analysis](/blog/uk-nightclub-closures-licensing-reform) and our [Europe closures tracker](/blog/europe-club-closures-tracker). The short version is that fees are one input among many.

## Why have fees gone up?

The evidence points to several overlapping causes, none of which is proven alone: festivals and sponsors bidding for a few reliable draws, agents who negotiate hard and sometimes demand exclusivity, social-media reach that makes small names bookable at scale, post-pandemic demand, higher touring costs, and a thin middle tier of artists. Each has a documented case and a weakness.

### Festivals and sponsors bidding for headliners

Documented: in the 2019 Mixmag feature Josh Doherty blamed sponsored festivals and well-funded promoters for setting fees that agents then expect everywhere. In 2015, Stutterheim linked EDM fees to what Las Vegas and Miami venues paid. In 2023, Resident Advisor reported that high-income markets such as Saudi Arabia and the UAE were normalising very high summer fees. In France, the CNM data show show-purchase costs at festivals with budgets above €3m rising 13% in 2024 against 3% more acts programmed.

Weakness: festivals are not obviously winning, and our [festival cancellations tracker](/blog/europe-festival-cancellations-2026) shows how many are struggling. The same CNM panel had two thirds of festivals in deficit in 2024, with an average result of minus €42,177, and festivals with budgets above €3m were the ones that tipped into majority deficit. Mor Elian, quoted by RA in 2023, noted that some festivals were barely breaking even despite strong ticket sales. A bidding war that leaves the bidders loss-making is a sign of mispricing, not of a market in balance. (If you want to see which festivals are still going, our list of [techno and house festivals with confirmed 2027 dates](/blog/best-techno-house-festivals-2027) is a start.) And in 2015, Tomorrowland's spokesperson argued that many DJs ask only standard fees there.

### Agents, exclusivity and radius clauses

Documented: Mixmag in 2019 reported that Sumner and Williams wanted exclusivity embargo deals lifted, describing them as often covering about six months either side of a date, and that agents frequently refused to negotiate. A radius or exclusivity clause, [as Wikipedia summarises it](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radius_clause), bars a performer from playing within a set distance and time of a booking, and supporters say it protects organisers' investments while critics say it discourages acts from playing smaller places. In Dazed's 2026 column, exclusivity is presented as a way of locking small venues out even when they could pay. The 2023 RA piece reported that a few large agencies reportedly use bidding wars and big rosters to raise prices, and that some promoters say agents are not always transparent about offers.

Weakness: the clearest reporting on exclusivity shows it is imposed by clubs and promoters as well. [Resident Advisor's November 2022 investigation](https://ra.co/news/78020) found clauses being used by venues and talent buyers to protect their own bookings, with windows of a week up to six weeks in London and eight in Berlin for one DJ, and the harm falling mostly on young, agentless local DJs who need regular gigs. One DJ in that piece, Ariel Zetina, argued that if a DJ is restricted the payment has to reflect it. In other words, exclusivity is also a cost that promoters ask artists to carry. Agents, for their part, have a defence on the record: one anonymous agent told Mixmag that agents face constant targets and artists can leave, and Rachael Williams argued that an agent who pushes for the best fee is doing the job well.

### Social media and algorithmic fame

Documented: Bagdai told Dazed that TikTok and social media let DJs skip the small-club circuit. Sumner, in 2019, said the move to festivals and superclubs made the scene more reliant on social media and PR teams. Our [piece on hard techno's US boom](/blog/hard-techno-american-boom) describes how younger audiences increasingly discover music through short-form video and algorithmic playlists rather than residencies.

Weakness: we found no data quantifying how much follower counts move fees, and the 2019 feature does not analyse the effect in depth. This is a plausible mechanism and a widely held belief, but it is not proven.

### Consolidation

Documented: KKR bought Superstruct for about €1.3bn in 2024, Superstruct bought Boiler Room in January 2025 and owns about 80 festival brands, and an AIF analysis puts Live Nation at 58% of UK primary tickets, all as reported in [our nightlife data piece](/blog/why-people-go-out-less-nightlife-data-2026) and our [Boiler Room piece](/blog/boiler-room-kkr-boycott). Dazed's column links big-company ticketing power to the squeeze on small venues.

Weakness: the Live Nation figure is about arenas and stadiums, and we found no evidence tying corporate consolidation to club-level DJ fees. It is a plausible background pressure. It is not demonstrated.

### Post-pandemic demand and travel costs

Documented: Resident Advisor in 2023 described a post-lockdown boom in demand. Daniel Howe's 2022 comment about artists asking for double fees fits the same pattern, and Stutterheim-era arguments about fees tracking US money show the pattern is not new. On the cost side, the UTA agent in the 2023 piece said travel was her clients' biggest issue, sometimes four to five times historical levels, and an anonymous touring DJ said flights for an Australian tour had roughly doubled. Our [Brexit touring analysis](/blog/brexit-touring-uk-dj-eu-festivals-2026) reports PRS data showing UK artists' performances at smaller general live music venues in Germany falling from about 5,000 in 2019 to about 3,600 in 2024.

Weakness: this explains why artists might ask for more, which is a different claim from showing that the asked-for fees are unreasonable. A DJ whose flights have doubled and whose tax paperwork has grown has a cost-based argument, and it is strongest for the artists at the bottom of the fee scale, not at the top.

### A thin middle tier and scarce headliners

Documented: Blackett and Ben Rau, a Berlin DJ and label owner interviewed by Mixmag in 2019, both described the middle tier as shrinking. Stutterheim's 10%-take-90% remark and Rau's reference to the top 1% taking a disproportionate share point the same way. Resident Advisor's database, per Attack Magazine, had 134,147 DJ profiles in May 2025, of which 2,175 (1.6%) had five or more upcoming bookings.

Weakness: the 1.6% figure is a count of profiles with many bookings, not a fee distribution. Scarcity of proven draws is a good economic explanation for high prices at the top, but we cannot show from public data that it is getting worse.

### Streaming income and fee pressure

Our own inference, flagged as such: if recorded-music income is small for most acts, live fees matter more to them and they have less reason to discount. The same squeeze runs through the label side, as our [study of independent label survival](/blog/independent-label-survival-study) shows. We have only partial evidence on the link to fees. [Resident Advisor reported in April 2024](https://ra.co/news/80514) that Spotify's rule of 1,000 streams in 12 months before a track earns royalties took effect on 1 April 2024. Spotify said tracks below the threshold account for 0.5% of streams and royalties, about $0.03 a month on average each, while the Union of Musicians and Allied Workers said 86% of tracks fall below it. The two sides are not measuring the same thing, and RA's article does not discuss electronic music specifically. GigXchange's report, for UK musicians broadly, says live gigs account for 40 to 60% of annual music income for a mid-tier gigging act. We found no study linking streaming income to DJ fee demands.

## Who gets squeezed?

The people with the least bargaining power: independent promoters who need a headliner to sell tickets, small clubs with thin margins, resident and local DJs whose slice of the budget shrinks, and audiences who pay for the result. Emerging DJs are squeezed from the other side, by a market that rewards proven draws.

**Promoters and small clubs.** The strongest documented cases are Sumner, who closed a four-year touring party in 2019, and Hegemann, whose Dortmund club introduced a fee cap in January 2026 and [announced on 8 October 2026](https://ra.co/news/85965) that it would close at the end of the year. We want to be careful here. Resident Advisor's report gives Hegemann's reasons as falling visitor numbers, rising costs and changing habits, with audiences drawn to summer festivals and individual event brands and DJs, and says an exclusively techno programme is no longer economically viable. The RA article does not mention artist fees or the cap. A club that capped fees and ran free-entry community nights still closed, which tells us that fee discipline alone does not rescue a room.

**Residents and local DJs.** If 80% of the budget goes to the headliner, as in Blackett's description, the local DJs split what is left. Mixmag's 2015 estimate put a fledgling local resident at often nothing or about £100 a night, and [RA reported in 2022](https://ra.co/news/78020) that local DJs in Tokyo were getting around $100 a show at small bars, needing roughly 30 gigs a month to earn what three larger gigs once paid. Those are anecdotes, but they point in one direction. Residents are also the group most exposed to exclusivity clauses, which in the RA 2022 piece fall hardest on young, agentless local DJs.

**Audiences.** Ticket price is the obvious route for fees to reach people, but the evidence is mixed. fabric's average weekend door price was flat between 2019 and 2022 per Vice. In October 2025, Sacha Lord wrote in City AM that entry at smaller clubs averages around £15, against up to £55 at large clubs once booking fees are included. Goldman Sachs' Music in the Air, [as summarised by World Music Views](https://worldmusicviews.com/goldman-sachs-live-music-shows-resilience-as-industry-set-to-grow-7-2-annually-through-2030/), found the average top-100 tour ticket rose from $91 in 2019 to $136 in 2024, but that is stadium and arena pricing, driven by superstar tours, not club nights. We did not find any source that directly links club ticket prices to DJ fees.

**Emerging DJs.** Toolroom Academy's April 2024 survey of more than 250 emerging artists, [summarised in our DJ economics piece](/blog/underground-dj-economics-data), found 76% did not think an electronic music career was financially sustainable and 82% relied on a job outside music. In Mixmag's 2019 piece, the Berlin DJ Eric Cloutier said he had taken a part-time job. Williams argued that stacked line-ups shut out smaller promoters, poorer promoters and people of colour, a point that echoes our [report on London licensing barriers for Black-led events](/blog/remel-black-music-events-london-licensing-study), though that study is about licensing, not fees.

## Is it breaking the underground's philosophy?

Partly, but the philosophy was never a pay structure. The documented ethos is about independence, anonymity and control over the music, and the early scenes had their own money problems; what is genuinely at stake is whether a local scene can afford to book its own.

**What the underground said about itself.** In a 1994 interview originally published by Jockey Slut and [reproduced in the Disco Pogo archive](https://www.discopogo.co/archive/underground-resistance-1994), Underground Resistance's Mike Banks said it was as much of an honour to stay underground as to be a commercial success, that he would not be reshaped by a company, and that he rarely gave interviews and disliked being photographed. That is a statement about independence and facelessness. It does not say anything about what a DJ should be paid.

**What the early scenes were like.** The Music Institute in Detroit is often cited as a founding room. [Red Bull Music Academy's 2017 history](https://daily.redbullmusicacademy.com/2017/05/music-institute-nightclubbing) reports that its co-founder George Baker wanted DJs to be in control of the music rather than answering to an owner or promoter, that it served no alcohol, and that its residents were unpaid at first. Derrick May said they played for free, for the love of it, for almost the first year, and later had to fight to get $400 a week. The club closed in November 1989; Baker said they could not pay the bills. The underground ethos there coexisted with unpaid artists and a club that could not make its numbers, which is a useful antidote to nostalgia.

**Where the philosophy and the fee debate genuinely meet.** Dazed's 2026 column argues that small rooms are where new sounds are made, citing DMZ at Mass in south London, Sheffield's bassline scene and Detroit's Music Institute, and that if DJs skip them the pipeline dries up. This is opinion, though it is a coherent one. Keira Sinclair, co-founder of the agency POLY., made the opposite point in 2019: that the best nights are the ones where the line-up does not matter, which is a philosophical stance a promoter can adopt without any change in anyone's fee. Rau argued in the same piece that headliners deserve to be paid, and that this should not stifle diverse line-ups.

**Where the thesis overreaches.** The claim that rising fees are breaking the underground assumes a baseline in which DJs were modestly and fairly paid. The 1996 Mixmag piece shows top DJs commanding large sums three decades ago and the Music Institute shows artists unpaid in the first place. A more defensible claim is that the relationship between the top fee and the average fee has widened, and that the scenes that depend on mid-level DJs and locals are the ones paying for it. For how one famous club is actually run, see our [explainer on Berghain and its operators](/blog/who-runs-berghain-ownership-operators), though it does not cover fees, and we found no public fee data for any of the clubs it discusses.

## What are promoters and artists doing about it?

A handful of documented experiments exist, and almost all are small, recent or only partly evidenced. The table below covers the ones we could verify, with what each does and where its limits are.

| Model | Who | What it does | Evidence of effect | Limits |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fee cap | Tresor.West, Dortmund | An upper limit on what the club pays any DJ, whatever their name or reach, from 1 Jan 2026 | None published; Mixmag gave no number and no artist reaction | The club announced its closure on 8 Oct 2026; RA does not link that to the cap |
| Free-entry community nights | Tresor.West | Unannounced line-ups with more local DJs, free entry, from January 2025 | Hegemann reported 650, 680 and 730 people on three Saturdays and a first break-even | Operator's own figures; the club later announced it would close |
| Sliding-scale fee | fabric, London | £4,000 base rising to £10,000 if sold out, against a £7,000 flat fee | Described by Mixmag in 2019; no results published | Needs trust and data from both sides; only described, never audited |
| Flat fee for everyone | STAUB at about:blank, Berlin; Gut Level, Sheffield | STAUB pays about €300 to every DJ and does not announce line-ups; Gut Level has an equal pay policy | Resident Advisor (2022) and Dazed (2026) report the policies; no financial data | Small rooms; scale unknown; Dazed gives no detail on Gut Level's terms |

Two observations. First, the models split into those that change what an artist is paid (caps, flat fees, sliding scales) and those that change who gets booked (community nights, no headliners, unannounced line-ups). The second group does not touch fee levels at all, it avoids the problem by not having a headliner. Second, none of these has published results that would let us say it works. Tresor.West's community nights drew well on three nights in early 2025 and the club still concluded, 20 months later, that its model was not viable.

## What is the artists' side?

Artists face a real cost squeeze of their own: agent and management commissions, travel that has become dearer, tax and permit paperwork after Brexit, and recorded income that is small for most. A DJ asking for a high fee is not, by default, doing something wrong.

**Commissions.** Rau told Mixmag in 2019 that agents take 15% and management 20%, so 35% is gone before the artist earns. Mixmag's 2015 earnings guide used the same range, 15 to 20% for each member of the team.

**Costs.** Mixmag's 2015 guide counted about £5,000 a year in flights and missed connections for a globally touring DJ in its third income tier, plus visas and studio costs, while noting that at the superstar tier promoters cover the expenses. In 2023, the agent quoted by RA said travel was her clients' biggest cost issue. Our [Brexit piece](/blog/brexit-touring-uk-dj-eu-festivals-2026) covers the paperwork and permits that now sit on top of small fees.

**Income.** The [Musicians' Census](https://www.helpmusicians.org.uk/media-and-press-office/first-ever-musicians-census-provides-unparalleled-insight-into-of-the-careers-of-the-uks-music-creators), run by Help Musicians and the Musicians' Union with nearly 6,000 UK respondents, found an average income from music of £20,700, 43% earning under £14,000 from music and 53% supplementing with income from outside music. That is all musicians, not DJs, and not only electronic. The IMS Business Report, [as reported by MusicTech](https://musictech.com/news/industry/ims-electronic-music-business-report-2026/), put the electronic music business at $15.1bn in 2025, up 7%. A growing sector in which most artists earn little is the shape you would expect from a market where income concentrates at the top.

**Career length.** The 2019 Mixmag feature says artists typically have a limited window, about three to five years, to build a career; it is a claim in the piece, not a measured statistic. If that is true, then a headline artist asking for what the market will pay while the window is open is doing what most freelancers would do.

**Fairness within the artist pool.** We found no study that quantifies a gender or ethnicity pay gap in club DJ fees. The nearest source is [Jezebel's 2019 summary of a New York Times feature](https://jezebel.com/surprise-women-djs-still-dont-have-equal-pay-1836664632), in which DJs and a booking collective described being underpaid and clubs raising offers when challenged; it is anecdotal, and the one number in it is a ceiling of about $2,000 an hour from one collective. We do not draw conclusions from it. The point stands that fairness is contested: Sinclair told Mixmag in 2019 that the word itself is loaded.

**What the fee buys.** An anonymous agent in the 2019 piece argued that fees reflect the crowds a DJ brings in. Rau agreed that headliners deserve pay because they draw people. This is the strongest counter-argument to the whole thesis: if a DJ does move 1,000 tickets, a high fee may be the market working properly. The complaint is strongest when a fee is set by reputation or by a festival's budget, not by the ticket sales at the room that has to pay it.

## What could a healthier fee culture look like?

These are RTS.FM's own suggestions, built on the sourced experiments above. They are not tested policy.

1. **Share risk by default.** fabric's sliding scale is the most developed example: a floor, a ceiling and a sell-out bonus. The artist keeps a guaranteed minimum, and the club does not pay the full price of a miss.
2. **Pay for exclusivity, or shorten it.** Zetina's point in the RA 2022 piece is fair: if a DJ is restricted, the fee should reflect it. Windows measured in weeks rather than months, case-by-case terms like Elsewhere's, and no clauses at all for local openers cost a promoter little and help the artists with the least leverage.
3. **Protect the local slot.** STAUB's flat €300 for every DJ, and Tresor.West's unannounced community nights, both break the link between a name and a fee. Local DJs who draw a crowd on their own, as Nesimi said they often do, deserve a fee that is not the leftover.
4. **Be more open about budgets.** Blackett said in 2019 that promoters in London already share information about bar spend and ticket sales. Sharing the aggregate figures with agents, in the way Keira Sinclair described agents asking for detailed budgets, would give both sides more to negotiate with than rumour.
5. **Treat venue costs as a policy problem too.** The Berlin and UK data in our [nightlife piece](/blog/why-people-go-out-less-nightlife-data-2026) show rent, rates, staff and bar supply costs weighing heavily. Pressing artists to charge less while venues absorb those costs shifts a structural problem onto the people with the least slack.
6. **Let those who can afford it take the first step.** Dazed's argument is that headline DJs and agents with leverage could waive or soften exclusivity and play a smaller room occasionally for a smaller fee. Hegemann made a similar point in 2025. It is voluntary, it depends on goodwill, and Hardy himself acknowledges that moral appeals are not a long-term fix.

## What we make of it

This section is RTS.FM's opinion, not reporting. Each point ends with the strongest counter-argument we could find.

1. **The thesis is right about direction and wrong about breadth.** Operators in several countries say artist fees are rising, and French festival data show it, but the sharpest inflation is at the top of the market, where a small number of acts can sell tickets at scale. For most working DJs, the evidence points to fees that are low, not unreasonable. *The strongest counter-argument is the NTIA and LiveKomm surveys: 61.5% and 70% of operators is not a small minority, and Blackett and Rau both describe the middle tier being squeezed, which implies the effect is not confined to a handful of stars.*

2. **Fees are a symptom as much as a cause.** High fees reflect scarce, proven draws, bidding by festivals and sponsors, and an attention economy that concentrates audiences on a few names. Meanwhile clubs close for rent, rates, energy, staff and shifting habits, as our [closures research](/blog/europe-club-closures-tracker) shows, and Tresor.West closed with a cap in place. *The strongest counter-argument is Mixmag's 2019 reporting on the 80% pattern: when one line takes most of a night's budget, it is the cost a promoter controls most directly, and Sumner closed his party because of it.*

3. **The underground's philosophy is worth defending, but not as a pay scale.** Its documented values are independence, anonymity and control over the music, and the early rooms had unpaid DJs and bills they could not meet. What deserves protection is the ability of a small room to book its own people and let a night stand on its sound. *The strongest counter-argument is that ethos is partly what promoters and audiences are buying: STAUB's flat fee and Tresor.West's cap are attempts to make the philosophy operational, and they only exist because someone thought it was in danger.*

4. **Artists should not carry the cost of a broken venue economy.** Most DJs earn modest sums, pay a third or more of a fee to agents and management, and face dearer travel. The fixes with the most promise are shared risk, shorter exclusivity and more open budgets, with public policy on rent and rates doing the structural work. *The strongest counter-argument, made in Dazed's column, is that the DJs with the most leverage have a responsibility to the scenes that made them, and that waiting for policy is not a plan for a promoter who needs to sell tickets this weekend.*

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much do DJs get paid per gig?

There is no reliable public average. Dated and anecdotal estimates exist: Mixmag's 2015 guide put a fledgling local resident at nothing or about £100 a night and an up-and-coming national DJ at £250 to £2,500 a gig, Resident Advisor reported in 2022 that local DJs in Tokyo were getting about $100 a show, and a Berlin party was paying a flat €300 to each DJ. Treat all of these as illustrations, not benchmarks.

### Why are DJ fees so high?

At the top of the market, fees reflect a small number of DJs who can sell tickets at scale, competition between festivals and well-funded promoters, agents who push for the best offer, and demand after the pandemic. Artists also have costs: agents and management can take about 35% in total, and travel has become more expensive. How much each factor matters has not been measured.

### How much does a headline DJ cost?

We found no credible current public figure. Older reports include Mixmag's 2015 estimate that superstars take at least £10,000 for a small venue and £20,000 for a festival main stage, a 2015 report of techno and tech house DJs asking €20,000 to €40,000 a set, and a €45,000 two-hour set mentioned in Mixmag in 2019. These are dated and some are second-hand, so we do not recommend using them as current prices.

### Are DJ fees rising?

Operators say so. A 2022 NTIA survey found 61.5% of 325 UK operators were paying more for artists and DJs than before the pandemic, and a 2025 German survey found 70% of 245 venues reporting rising fee demands. French festival data show costs of buying shows rising faster than inflation. There is no fee index, so the size of the rise is unknown.

### How do club promoters pay DJs?

Usually a flat fee agreed in advance, often through an agent. Variations include a lower base fee with a bonus if the night sells out, as fabric described in 2019, and shares of door income. We could not find a primary source on how common each type is in club techno.

### What is a radius clause?

A contract term that stops an artist playing within a set distance and time of a booking. In club DJ culture the same idea is usually called an exclusivity or embargo clause, and it often covers a number of weeks or months either side of a gig. Supporters say it protects the promoter's investment; critics say it blocks smaller promoters and local DJs.

### Do DJs get paid door splits?

Sometimes. A door split pays the artist a share of ticket income instead of, or on top of, a fixed fee. Pay then rises or falls with attendance. A 2011 CD Baby article said splits of 80 to 20 in the performer's favour are common for bands, but we found no reliable figure for club DJs, so we cannot say how typical any percentage is.

### How much do techno DJs earn?

We found no techno-specific earnings survey. For UK musicians generally, the Musicians' Census found an average income from music of £20,700, with 43% earning under £14,000. A 2024 Toolroom Academy survey of emerging electronic artists found 76% did not think the career was financially sustainable and 82% relied on outside work. Headline techno DJs can earn much more, but no verified figure is public.

### Are DJ fees the reason clubs are closing?

Not on the evidence we have. Artist fees are one cost among several. Berlin clubs surveyed in 2026 named staff, overheads, falling guest spending power and rent as the main pressures, and artist fees were not reported in the summaries we read. Tresor.West introduced a fee cap in January 2026 and announced in October that it would close at the end of the year, citing visitor numbers, costs and changing habits.

### Do any clubs cap DJ fees?

At least one has said so publicly. Tresor.West in Dortmund announced an upper limit on fees from 1 January 2026 that applies to all artists, though it published no figure. Other approaches include flat fees for every DJ and sliding scales tied to ticket sales. None of these has published results.

We read, between 15 and 18 October 2026, the articles and datasets linked above, in full or in the extract the page allowed: Mixmag (1996 via 909originals, 2015, 2019, 2025 and 2026), Resident Advisor (2022, 2023, 2024 and 2026), Dazed (August 2026), Vice (2022), City AM (2025), Hypebot (2025), Dancing Astronaut and Event Planner (2015), Pollstar (2026), MIZ for LiveKomm (2025), the Clubcommission, the French CNM festival economy report, the Help Musicians Musicians' Census release, Red Bull Music Academy, the Disco Pogo archive of a 1994 Jockey Slut interview, Jezebel, MusicTech, World Music Views, Attack Magazine and GigXchange. For our own earlier posts we reused only figures that carry a source we could open ourselves.

Long pages were read through an automated text-extraction tool, so we paraphrase rather than quote, and the CNM PDF was read from its extracted text.

**Blocked or not opened.** DJ Mag, IQ Magazine, the Complete Music Update archive, Le Monde, Fourvenues and the Goldman Sachs PDF itself returned errors or blocks, so we did not cite them; where a figure came from Goldman Sachs we cite a secondary summary and say so. A Live Music Exchange post by promoter Gareth Whitehead, on whether DJ fees are killing dance music, returned a server error, so we have not used it. We did not open Billboard or The Guardian, and a vendor blog from Ticket Fairy that repeats a French "30 to 40%" fee-rise claim linked to a Le Monde article we could not open, so we left that claim out and used the CNM figures instead. A FestivalPro article on widening artist fees carried no data, and Attack Magazine's 2014 LEME panel page listed rising DJ fees as a topic without giving any statements, so neither is relied on.

**Gaps.** No source gave current fee levels for any named DJ that we could verify. The Dazed column cites an unverified online rumour of a seven-figure fee for one named artist; we do not repeat it. We found no agent responding on the record to the Dazed column, no quantified link between social-media following and fees, and no study of a gender or ethnicity pay gap in club fees.

**A correction flag.** Our [earlier DJ economics piece](/blog/underground-dj-economics-data) cited a GigXchange band of £100 to £400 for an evening DJ slot, with a sample of 4,191 observations. The PDF text we could extract does not contain that band or that sample size, so we have not repeated it here. We corrected that article on 18 October 2026 and removed the band, the sample size and two other unsupported figures.

## What we could not verify

- Any current public fee for a named headline DJ, and the seven-figure rumour cited in Dazed, which rests on an unverified online post.
- Whether the 80% headliner share is typical anywhere beyond the examples Mixmag described in 2019.
- The size of the fee rise since 2019 in clubs, as opposed to festivals. The French data cover contemporary music festivals, not electronic clubs.
- Whether Tresor.West's fee cap changed the club's finances, which artists accepted it, and what the cap is. The cap's level was not published.
- Whether Secret Garden Party dropped headliners as it pledged, and whether Grassroots Rising took place or saved 30 to 40%.
- How Gut Level's equal pay policy works in detail.
- Whether social-media following measurably raises fees.
- Whether consolidation in festivals and ticketing raises club-level fees.
- The typical size of agent commission in club techno today. Rau's 15% and 20% figures date from 2019, and a 2015 Mixmag guide gave the same range.
- A gender or ethnicity pay gap in club DJ fees, which we found no study of.
- The share of DJs or promoters on door-split or versus deals.

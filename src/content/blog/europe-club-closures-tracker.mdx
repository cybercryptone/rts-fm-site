![The exterior of Wilde Renate, a former apartment building in Berlin's Friedrichshain district](https://rts.fm/images/commons/Wilde_Renate_Berlin.jpg)

Ask ten people in dance music whether clubs are closing faster than they used to, and you'll get ten confident answers and roughly zero comparable numbers behind them. Part of the problem is real: most cities don't measure this. There's no EU-wide registry of nightclub openings and closures, no standardized reporting requirement, and no single body whose job it is to count. What exists instead is a patchwork, a serious annual survey in one city, a scattered pile of press clippings in another, and nothing at all in most.

This piece is an attempt to put what's actually documented in one place. We've already covered [why the UK keeps losing its nightclubs](/blog/uk-nightclub-closures-licensing-reform) in depth, business rates, licensing policy and the Agent of Change gap, and separately [the wave of European festival cancellations](/blog/europe-festival-cancellations-2026) hitting outdoor events for a related but distinct set of reasons. Neither of those pieces set out to be a cross-city club tracker, so this one does: five cities, the real published numbers where they exist, and an honest account of where they don't.

Berlin comes first, because it has the only data here that actually deserves the word rigorous.

## Berlin: the worst year since 2009, backed by an actual survey

Clubcommission Berlin's Club Culture Berlin 2026 report, the first major study of the city's club scene since 2019, surveyed 102 venues and promoters. The headline number is stark: 28 clubs closed against only 7 new openings in 2025, a net loss of 21 clubs. That's the worst single year for Berlin's club scene since 2009.

Context matters here, and the report supplies it. Zoom out to the longer run since 2020, and the picture had actually held roughly steady, 24 clubs closed but 25 opened across those years, close to net neutral. 2025 is the year that broke that trend, not a continuation of a slow bleed that had been running the whole time.

The financial numbers underneath the closure count explain why. Only 61 percent of Berlin clubs and promoters broke even in 2025, down from 79 percent in 2017. Almost four in ten surveyed venues, 39 percent, reported operating at a loss in 2025, versus 21 percent in 2017. And looking forward, almost one in four surveyed clubs, 23 percent, said they're considering giving up within the next 12 months. That last figure is the one worth sitting with: this isn't only a count of what already happened, it's a signal that the 2025 number may not be the low point.

Some of the closures behind these statistics have names attached. Watergate, one of Berlin's most internationally recognized rooms, shut permanently at the end of 2024 after 22 years. SchwuZ, the city's oldest queer club, filed for bankruptcy in 2024. And [Wilde Renate, one of the city's most distinctive DIY clubs](/blog/wilde-renate-berlin-club-history), came within months of losing its building entirely before securing a new ten-year lease, a genuine reversal in a scene where most stories run the other direction. We've also cited the Clubcommission's numbers briefly in [our look at Berghain's fall in DJ Mag's 2026 poll](/blog/berghain-unvrs-dj-mag-top-100), where the same 28-versus-7 figures showed up as context for why even the world's most famous techno club is operating inside a contracting scene.

The pattern isn't confined to Berlin, either. Munich lost [BLITZ, one of Germany's most respected small-capacity techno rooms](/blog/blitz-munich-closure), in 2026, a club with no direct lease dispute or redevelopment plan cited publicly, just "reasons beyond our control" after a lease signed nearly a decade earlier ran up against today's rental market. Local media in Germany has taken to calling the broader trend Clubsterben, club death, and Berlin's structured annual survey is currently the only place in Europe where you can see that trend measured with anything close to rigor.

## What about the UK?

We're deliberately keeping this section short, because [our UK piece](/blog/uk-nightclub-closures-licensing-reform) already covers the club-specific story there in full: business rates relief that excludes nightclubs, Cumulative Impact Policies freezing new licenses in specific boroughs, and the Agent of Change gap between planning law and licensing law. What belongs here is the adjacent context. The Association of Independent Festivals recorded 78 UK festivals cancelled, postponed or permanently closed in 2024, 43 in 2025, and 20 more by June 10, 2026, a partial-year count. That's festival data, not club data, but it points at the same underlying cost squeeze on live electronic music showing up on a different calendar. Not every UK venue story runs one direction, either: [Printworks in London closed in 2023 and is reopening in 2026](/blog/printworks-london-reopening-2026), a rare closure-to-reopening arc worth remembering whenever the wider trend starts to feel inevitable.

## Netherlands: a festival permit story with a club-adjacent twist

The Netherlands doesn't have a citywide club closure count in this tracker either, and we're not going to invent one. What we do have, again from festival-side reporting, is that roughly 50 of the more than 100 global festival cancellations in 2025 happened in the Netherlands alone, a striking number for a country that built some of dance music's most important stages over the past two decades.

The one genuinely new data point for this piece is Music On. Its Amsterdam festival was cancelled at the gate in May 2026 after the permit was revoked over structural safety concerns, a regulatory shutdown rather than a financial one. The same year, the same brand's residency at Pacha Ibiza ran a thriving two-part Grand Closing Weekend, business as usual for one of the island's flagship club nights. Same name, same year, opposite outcomes, a clean illustration of how differently a club residency and an outdoor festival permit are weathering risk right now. [Our festival cancellations piece](/blog/europe-festival-cancellations-2026) covers the Music On story in full; it's mentioned here only for that contrast.

## Paris: no citywide count, but a landmark closure

Here's where we have to be honest about a real gap. No comprehensive citywide closure count for Paris turned up in our research, and we're not going to paper over that with an invented aggregate. What is documented: Paris and greater Ile-de-France have around 350 clubs and discos total, with roughly 200 of those inside Paris proper. That's the denominator. Nobody appears to be publishing the numerator, the way Clubcommission does for Berlin.

What we can report with confidence is one specific, significant loss. Gibus, a club on Faubourg-du-Temple that had been a cornerstone of the city's LGBTQIA+ and music nightlife since 1967, permanently closed in summer 2026. The venue cited operating costs, noise tensions with neighbors, and falling attendance, the same trio of pressures showing up in Berlin and London under different regulatory names. Fifty-nine years is a long run for any club anywhere, and its closure is a real marker even without a citywide count to place it against.

Worth a brief aside, a different category entirely: Parisian gaming clubs faced a closure threat starting January 1, 2025, after a finance bill that would have made their authorization permanent for 2026 was rejected and the Prime Minister resigned. It's a reminder of the regulatory instability the city's licensed nightlife sector has had to absorb, not a data point for this tracker.

## The United States: named closures, no national count

If Paris has a thin data picture, the US has essentially none. There is no single comprehensive nationwide closure count for American nightclubs, so we won't invent one. What exists instead is a pile of individually reported closures that made 2025 a widely reported rough year for clubs and live venues across the country.

The named cases span coasts and scales. Yolo in San Francisco was one of two SF clubs that announced closing in 2025. Brooklyn and Los Angeles both saw venue losses, including the Mayan Theater in LA, a legendary former movie palace. Milwaukee lost Victor's after 60 years in business. Further closures were reported in Chicago, Cleveland, San Diego, and Neptune, New Jersey.

The National Independent Venue Association, NIVA, which represents roughly 1,500 US clubs, concert halls, performing arts centers and festivals, has become the main industry voice tracking the pressure on this list, citing rising rents, rising insurance costs, and declining alcohol consumption as the core drivers. That last factor, falling alcohol sales, doesn't show up as prominently in the European reporting above, and it's worth flagging as a possible US-specific wrinkle rather than assuming every market is under exactly the same pressure for exactly the same reasons.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Which city has the most reliable club closure data?

Berlin, by a clear margin. Clubcommission Berlin's Club Culture Berlin 2026 report surveyed 102 venues and promoters directly, producing both a closure count (28 closed, 7 opened in 2025) and financial detail (break-even rates, share operating at a loss) that no other city in this tracker has published. It's the only dataset here built from a structured annual survey rather than press monitoring.

### Is this the same story as the UK's nightclub closures?

Related, but this piece deliberately doesn't repeat it. [Our UK piece](/blog/uk-nightclub-closures-licensing-reform) covers the specific mechanics there, business rates relief that excludes nightclubs, Cumulative Impact Policies, and the Agent of Change gap between planning and licensing law. This tracker cites the UK's festival cancellation numbers only as brief context for the country's wider live-music cost squeeze.

### How is a club closure different from a festival cancellation?

They're different formats with different pressure points. A club is a fixed venue facing rent, business rates, licensing renewal and noise complaints over years or decades. A festival is a temporary event facing insurance costs, weather exposure and one-off permitting for a single edition. [Our festival cancellations piece](/blog/europe-festival-cancellations-2026) covers that side in depth, including cases like Music On's Amsterdam permit revocation, which sits closer to the festival category even though it shares a brand name with a thriving club residency at Pacha Ibiza.

### Why isn't there a single Europe-wide club closure number?

Because nobody is currently measuring it at that scale. Berlin has Clubcommission's structured survey. Paris has a rough total venue count (around 350 across Ile-de-France) but no published annual closure tally. The Netherlands and UK numbers cited here come from festival trackers, not club-specific ones. The US has NIVA's advocacy but no comprehensive national count either. Aggregating those into one continent-wide figure would mean averaging real data with gaps, which is exactly the kind of number this tracker is trying not to invent.

### Will this tracker get updated as more data comes in?

That's the intent. A tracker like this is only as good as what gets publicly reported and measured, and right now that's wildly uneven, one rigorous annual survey in Berlin, a handful of named closures everywhere else. If more cities start publishing Clubcommission-style structured data, this is the piece we'd update first.

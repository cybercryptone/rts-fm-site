Harvey McKay, the Glasgow DJ and producer whose driving, soulful techno made him one of Drumcode's most consistent hitmakers, has died. His death was reported on May 21, 2026, confirmed in a statement from Adam Beyer's Drumcode label. No cause of death has been disclosed, and none should be speculated on here.

For anyone who has spent time on a Drumcode-adjacent dancefloor over the past decade, McKay's name will be familiar even if his face wasn't always front of mind. He was a producer's producer: reliable, technically sharp, and rarely chasing the spotlight, which is part of why tributes from across the international techno community, from [Resident Advisor](https://ra.co/news/85255) to [DJ Mag](https://djmag.com/news/harvey-mckay-scottish-techno-dj-and-producer-has-died), have landed with such weight this week.

## From Glasgow's Underground to the International Circuit

McKay's roots were in Glasgow's club underground, a scene long defined by the Sub Club, Slam, and a culture that prized substance over spectacle. His own DJ history stretched back to the early 1990s, cutting his teeth in that environment well before his production work began to travel beyond Scotland.

It was in the 2000s that McKay's studio output started to draw wider attention. Early records for Perc's Perc Trax and Saytek's Cubism, alongside releases on Slam's own Soma Quality Recordings, positioned him within a lineage of Scottish techno that has always punched above its weight internationally, a lineage that runs through Slam, Silicone Soul, and the Sub Club residencies that shaped a generation of Glasgow producers. By the late 2000s he had built a reputation as a peak-time producer whose tracks worked as well in a warehouse as they did on a festival main stage.

That reputation translated into a heavy touring schedule. Over the following years McKay played Awakenings, Fabric, Creamfields, Studio 338, Rotterdam Rave, Uebel and Gefahrlich, and Fabrik, closed the opening night of Loveland Weekender, played a farewell season at Space Ibiza, and took his sound on a first tour of Australia. A show at London's Starlane Pizza Bar had reportedly been scheduled for the month following his death.

## A Driving, Soulful Sound Across a Deep Catalog

Where McKay distinguished himself was in a sound that critics and peers repeatedly described as driving and soulful, techno with groove and warmth rather than pure blunt force, though never lacking in weight on a big system. Alan Fitzpatrick, a longtime peer and fellow Drumcode artist, put it plainly in his tribute: "His music had the ability to slap you in the face, it was raw and full of energy."

His discography backs that up. McKay's first release for Drumcode came in 2013 with "Lost," the beginning of a relationship that would define much of his career. He followed it with "Silk Road" in 2014, and the label went on to carry a run of his EPs across the following years, including "The Illusion Of Control," "Black Dolphin," and "Glasgow Safari," a title that doubled as a nod to home. Tracks like "Venom," "Trick Baby," and "The Mad Drummer" became staples of his catalog and, by extension, of Drumcode sets worldwide.

Commercially, McKay was one of the more successful techno producers of his generation on Beatport, racking up multiple genre No.1s and a string of top 10 releases through the 2010s, output that at one point placed him among Beatport techno's best-selling artists of the year. Beyond Drumcode, he kept building relationships with other serious techno and tech house institutions: Bedrock, Cocoon, Rekids, Intec, and We Are The Brave, which released his "Set Back" EP in 2018. As recently as last year he had a single out on Cocoon, and he continued to record for imprints like Hardgroove, proof that his output never really slowed.

## The Drumcode Relationship and Faction

Drumcode was, without question, the label most associated with McKay's career, and the label's own tribute reflected that closeness. In its statement announcing his passing, Drumcode wrote: "Across his releases on Drumcode, Harvey brought a powerful and distinctive sound that left a lasting mark on the label and the wider techno scene. His music, energy and presence will not be forgotten."

That relationship went both directions. McKay wasn't just a name on Adam Beyer's roster, he was part of the Drumcode identity for over a decade, someone whose EPs helped define what the label's harder, groove-driven side sounded like through the 2010s and into the 2020s. Alongside that main partnership, McKay ran his own imprint, Faction, a smaller, artist-led outlet that let him release music on his own terms outside the pressures of a bigger label calendar. It was a common move for producers of his stature and generation, but it also said something about McKay's independence: even as one of Drumcode's most reliable names, he kept a foothold that was entirely his own.

## Tributes From the Techno Community

The scale of the response since news broke has underlined how deeply McKay was respected within the scene, not just as a hitmaker but as a person. Alongside Drumcode's statement and Fitzpatrick's tribute, artists including Pig and Dan, Dubfire, Bart Skils, Secret Cinema, Sara Landry, and Anja Schneider all paid their respects publicly in the days following the announcement.

Fellow Glasgow scene figure Fergie offered perhaps the most personal read on McKay's standing within Scottish techno specifically, describing him as someone who worked apart from the industry's usual attention-seeking: "He's out there on his own and always has been... he doesn't do it for the credit." It's a description that tracks with everything else said about McKay this week: a producer whose reputation was built on the strength of his records and his studio work ethic, not on self-promotion.

That combination, serious craft paired with a lack of interest in the spotlight, is part of what made McKay a respected figure across a genre that can be quick to burn out its stars. He was, by every account from his peers, in it for the music, a working producer who put out records rather than chasing headlines, and it is that body of work, not any single moment of fame, that the techno world is now sitting with.

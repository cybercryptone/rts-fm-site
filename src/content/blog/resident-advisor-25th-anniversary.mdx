There's a particular kind of vertigo that sets in when the outlet that has spent your entire adult life reviewing the records and running the news you actually trust turns around and becomes the subject of its own coverage. Resident Advisor, founded in Sydney in 2001 and long since the closest thing electronic music has to a paper of record, is 25 this year. It's marking the milestone the way a media outlet with that much institutional memory would: not with a single flashy event, but with a book that tries to make sense of what it's actually documented, and a ten date world tour that closes, deliberately, back where it all began.

The two initiatives were announced together, as reported by [RA itself](https://ra.co/news/85642) and [ra.co](https://ra.co/news/85643), and they read less like a marketing push than an outlet checking its own pulse. A publication that has covered club culture's watershed moments as they happened is now old enough to have watershed moments of its own worth revisiting. That's a strange position for a music publication to be in, and RA seems aware of it, which is part of what makes the anniversary worth paying attention to beyond the obvious "happy birthday" angle.

## What is Front Left, RA's new anthology book?

"Front Left: 25 Years of Electronic Music From the Future" is only RA's second book publication in its history, according to [RA's announcement](https://ra.co/news/85642), which alone says something about how selectively the outlet treats its own archive. It collects more than two decades of RA journalism, organized into four chapters: "Club Culture's Watershed Moments," "Around the World in Ten Dance Floors," "The Modern Architects," and "The Big Themes," a structure that's basically a table of contents for how RA has always framed its coverage, moments, geography, people, and ideas, now bound together as a single retrospective.

The book has a foreword from British artist Mark Leckey, whose own work has long engaged with rave culture and its afterlife, plus chapter essays from a genuinely varied set of contributors: DJ Call Super, Frankie Decaiza Hutchinson (cofounder of the queer and inclusive party series dweller), music industry researcher and educator Cherie Hu, and Sam Valenti IV, founder of Ghostly International. That lineup spans a working DJ, a party organizer building inclusive spaces, an academic tracking the industry's business side, and a label boss, fitting RA's stated ambition of the book documenting "the scenes, sounds, artists and ideas" that shaped club culture rather than just its charts and reviews.

Design comes from the New York studio 12:01 AM, with art direction by Hassan Rahim, and the book is published by Velocity Press with additional distribution through KD Presse. Per RA's own reporting, it's due out later in 2026 and is available for pre-order now. Neither a firm release date nor a price has been confirmed publicly as of this writing, so treat both as pending rather than fixed.

## Where is the RA25 world tour going, and why does the route matter?

The other half of the anniversary is a ten date run of parties, live shows and community events stretching from late September through early December 2026, [as detailed by RA](https://ra.co/news/85643):

- September 25: London, Outernet Live, in partnership with AVA Festival
- October 3: Tokyo, HVEN
- October 8: Taipei, Pawnshop
- October 9: New York City, Good Room
- October 17: Mexico City, Fünk
- October 22: Amsterdam, Bar San Francisco, a free Greenhouse Sessions event held during Amsterdam Dance Event
- November 7: Dublin, Yamamori Tengu
- November 12: Paris, essaim
- November 21: Berlin, a multi-venue takeover across Tresor, Globus and OHM
- December 5: Sydney, Club 77

Read as a route rather than a list, the tour maps almost exactly onto where RA's coverage has gone deepest over 25 years: the UK scene it grew up covering, the Berlin axis it treats as a second home, Tokyo and Taipei representing scenes RA has worked harder than most Western outlets to take seriously, Mexico City for Latin America's fast-growing underground, and Amsterdam timed to land inside ADE, the industry's own annual gathering. The Berlin stop alone, spread across Tresor, Globus and OHM in a single takeover, says something about how embedded RA has become in that city's infrastructure rather than just its guest list.

The Sydney date on December 5 is the one doing the real symbolic work. RA itself describes the finale as closing "a loop from 2026 to RA's founding in 2001," and the framing isn't subtle: a platform that has spent a quarter century reporting on scenes everywhere else is ending its anniversary year by returning to the room, city, and scene it actually came out of. Club 77 isn't a random Sydney venue booking, it's the closing bracket on a sentence RA started writing in 2001.

## Why does an anniversary matter for a music journalism outlet specifically?

Anniversaries are common currency in electronic music right now. RTS.FM has covered plenty of them this year alone, from [Cocoon Recordings turning 25](/blog/cocoon-recordings-25-years) to [Kompakt's long run in Cologne](/blog/kompakt-records-cologne-history) to [Rawax's story under Robert Drewek](/blog/rawax-records-robert-drewek). What makes RA's 25th a slightly different animal is that RA isn't a label or a club, it's the infrastructure that writes those other anniversaries into the record in the first place. When Cocoon or Kompakt hits a milestone, RA is frequently the outlet whose reporting frames how the rest of the industry, and outlets like this one, understand what happened.

That distinction, between an outlet that curates the historical record and one that gets voted on, is worth sitting with. RA's approach across 25 years has been editorially driven: staff writers and commissioned essayists like Call Super and Cherie Hu shaping which scenes and artists get the "watershed moment" treatment. That's a fundamentally different model from something like the [DJ Mag Top 100](/blog/nina-kraviz-dj-mag-top-100-2026-poll), where public voting decides who gets ranked and whose career gets that particular kind of visibility bump. Neither model is inherently more honest, but they answer "who decides what matters in electronic music" very differently, and RA's anniversary book is essentially a quarter century of that editorial model laid out in one volume.

## What does RA's independence look like next to a platform like Boiler Room?

It's also worth putting RA's 25th next to a very different anniversary-adjacent story from this year: [Boiler Room's ongoing boycott over its KKR-linked ownership](/blog/boiler-room-kkr-boycott). Boiler Room built its reputation on the same kind of underground trust RA has, streaming sets out of warehouses since 2010, before being sold to Superstruct Entertainment in January 2025, a festival conglomerate majority owned by the American private equity firm KKR. That ownership chain has produced a founder's exit, canceled shows, and organized protests through 2026, precisely because a platform's editorial identity and its ownership structure aren't actually separable once outside capital enters the picture.

RA, by contrast, remains an independent, editorially driven outlet marking 25 years entirely on its own terms, with no acquisition story, no private equity backer, and no boycott campaign attached to its anniversary. Who gets to shape electronic music's public record, an outlet answerable to its own editorial staff, or one answerable to a shareholder three ownership layers removed from the dance floor, is exactly the question Boiler Room's last two years have forced into the open. RA turning 25 without that shadow hanging over it is itself part of the story.

## How does this connect to the labels and scenes RA has covered?

RA's fingerprints are all over the kind of label history RTS.FM covers regularly. Its journalism on Kompakt's Cologne roots, its reporting on Cocoon's Ibiza residency moves, and its coverage of smaller imprints like Rawax have functioned as a shared reference point for how the wider industry, including outlets like this one, talks about those institutions. "The Modern Architects," one of Front Left's four chapters, is presumably where a lot of that label and producer history gets synthesized into something closer to a canon. Whether readers agree with every call RA has made over 25 years is beside the point. The fact that there's now a physical book consolidating those calls into a single, citable object is itself a milestone for how electronic music writing gets treated as a historical record rather than disposable coverage.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is Resident Advisor's Front Left book about?

"Front Left: 25 Years of Electronic Music From the Future" is RA's second-ever book publication, collecting more than two decades of its journalism across four chapters covering club culture's watershed moments, global dance floors, key artists and producers, and broader industry themes. It features a foreword by Mark Leckey and essays from Call Super, Frankie Decaiza Hutchinson, Cherie Hu and Sam Valenti IV.

### When does Front Left come out and how much does it cost?

RA has confirmed the book is available for pre-order now, with a full release due later in 2026. A specific release date and price have not been publicly confirmed as of this writing.

### What is the RA25 world tour and where is it happening?

RA25 is a ten date tour of parties, live shows and community events running from September 25, 2026 in London through December 5, 2026 in Sydney. Stops include Tokyo, Taipei, New York, Mexico City, Amsterdam during ADE, Dublin, Paris, and a multi-venue Berlin takeover across Tresor, Globus and OHM.

### Why is the tour ending in Sydney?

Sydney is where Resident Advisor was founded in 2001. RA has described the December 5 finale at Club 77 as closing "a loop from 2026 to RA's founding in 2001," making the tour's final date a deliberate return to the platform's origin city rather than an arbitrary stop.

### How is Resident Advisor different from platforms like Boiler Room or DJ Mag's rankings?

RA operates as an independent, editorially driven outlet, its coverage and now its anniversary book are shaped by staff and commissioned contributors rather than public votes or outside ownership. That distinguishes it from voted formats like the DJ Mag Top 100 and from platforms like Boiler Room, which was sold to KKR-backed Superstruct Entertainment in January 2025 and has faced an ongoing boycott campaign over that ownership since.

Ask most people to picture a working DJ's income and they'll picture the wrong one. They'll picture a name they've actually heard of, playing a room they've actually heard of, for a fee that sounds implausible until you remember festival headliners exist for a reason. That image isn't fake, but it's also not remotely representative, and until recently the gap between the visible top tier and everyone else was mostly anecdotal, promoters comparing notes, DJs comparing notes, nobody with a dataset big enough to say something definitive.

That's changed. Over the past couple of years, three separate organizations, working independently, with different methods and purposes, have published real numbers on DJ and electronic-musician economics. Resident Advisor's own booking database was analyzed by Attack Magazine simply to see how many profiles were actually working. GigXchange published a UK musician earnings report drawing on UK Music, Help Musicians, Musicians' Union and other published sources. Toolroom Academy surveyed emerging artists about sustainability, not fees. Put the three next to each other, and they land on the same conclusion from three different angles: a very small slice of DJs are working at a frequency that could plausibly be a living, there is no public DJ fee index, the best general figures on UK musician earnings are modest, and most emerging artists don't believe the career is financially sustainable at all.

This isn't RTS.FM's own survey. We haven't polled our own DJs or listeners here. What follows is a synthesis of three published, external datasets, credited by name throughout.

## How many DJs are actually working?

Start with the largest dataset of the three, because it's the one that measures scale rather than sentiment. Attack Magazine's June 2025 piece, ["The 1% Reality: What the Numbers Tell Us About DJ Economics,"](https://www.attackmagazine.com/features/long-read/the-1-reality-what-the-numbers-tell-us-about-dj-economics/) pulled directly from Resident Advisor's own booking database, arguably the most complete public record of who's actually gigging in club and festival culture.

As of May 26, 2025, RA's database held 134,147 total DJ profiles. Of those, only 21,351, about 16 percent, had even a single future booking listed. That's already a steep drop-off: five out of six profiles in RA's system show no upcoming show at all. But the number that gives the piece its title is further down the funnel. Just 2,175 DJs, 1.6 percent of every profile in the database, had five or more upcoming shows booked.

Five-plus upcoming bookings isn't an arbitrary bar. It's roughly the frequency you'd need to be working at something resembling a touring cadence, the kind of calendar that could plausibly generate a full-time income rather than the occasional local slot. By that measure, out of the entire universe of DJs with a public RA profile, only a sliver clear the line. Everyone else, the overwhelming majority, is either between bookings, playing occasionally, or not currently gigging at a frequency the data can even register as active.

## What does a working DJ actually get paid?

So say you clear that bar. You're one of the roughly 1 in 60 DJs on RA with a real booking calendar. What does that calendar actually pay?

GigXchange's report, ["UK Musician Earnings 2026: The GBP 8bn Paradox, the Income Ladder and the Access Gap,"](https://gigxchange.app/rates/reports/musician-earnings-2026.pdf) (April 2026, published under a CC BY 4.0 licence) is the most useful general source on pay in this market, with an important limit: it covers UK musicians as a whole, not DJs. We re-read the report text for this update and found no DJ fee band, so there is no published figure here for what a club DJ is paid per night, and we have removed the one this piece first carried.

What the report does give is context. It puts typical UK musician income at GBP 20,700, says 43 percent of musicians earn under GBP 14,000 a year, and that more than half need a second job, drawing on UK Music, Help Musicians and Musicians' Union data. It puts session dates at GBP 180 to GBP 400 per session at Musicians' Union rates, which is the nearest published benchmark for paid evening work, though session musicians are not DJs. And it says London-based musicians average around GBP 30,000 against the GBP 20,700 national figure, but that London's cost of living swallows most of the gap.

None of these figures is a DJ fee. A DJ-specific benchmark does not exist in the sources we could verify, and we are keeping it that way rather than printing a number we cannot source. For the debate about fees at the top of the market, see our later piece on [whether DJ fees are breaking the underground](/blog/dj-fees-underground-promoters-squeeze-2026), which documents what promoters have actually said and what the evidence does and does not show.

None of these could be traced to the GigXchange report or to any source we could open, so they have been removed.*

## Do emerging artists believe this is a sustainable career?

The third dataset answers a different question entirely: not how many people are booking, and not what they're paid, but whether the people trying to build a career actually believe it's viable.

Toolroom Academy's April 2024 report, ["From Mix to Mainstage: The Blueprint for Emerging Electronic Artists,"](https://admin.toolroomacademy.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/04/From-Mix-to-Mainstage.pdf) researched in partnership with Audience Strategies, surveyed more than 250 "new," emerging electronic artists and supplemented that with interviews across more established artists, educators, and industry professionals.

The headline finding is blunt: 76 percent of surveyed new artists said a career in electronic music is not financially sustainable. That's not a minority grumbling, that's more than three in four of the people actively trying to build this career telling researchers, on the record, that the economics don't currently work.

The knock-on numbers explain why. 82 percent of surveyed artists rely on a job outside music to support themselves, split between 56 percent working that job full-time and 26 percent part-time. And separate from income entirely, over 88 percent said growing an online audience is a struggle, which matters because audience size is increasingly the thing bookers and promoters use as a proxy for whether an artist can sell tickets, which in turn feeds back into who gets booked at all.

Read against RA's booking data and GigXchange's earnings data, this isn't a contradiction, it's the same picture from the inside. The people in Toolroom Academy's survey are, statistically, mostly the same population sitting outside RA's 1.6 percent, not booking at a touring frequency, working day jobs to cover the gap, and telling researchers directly they don't see a sustainable path from here.

## So who's actually capturing the money?

None of these three datasets were built to answer that question directly, but the shape of the answer follows from stacking them. If only 1.6 percent of DJ profiles are booking at a plausible full-time frequency, and even the working majority within that group is earning close to session-musician minimums, and 82 percent of emerging artists need a day job to make it work at all, then the revenue generated by the scene, streaming, tickets, merch, festival grosses, is being captured overwhelmingly by a small number of acts and the businesses built around them, not distributed across the base of people actually producing the culture. It's the same dynamic that shows up on [the label side of the business](/blog/independent-labels-market-share-2026), where "independent" doesn't automatically mean the money reaches more people, just that it's routed through different gatekeepers. Where the physical money in the wider scene does visibly flow, [vinyl sales hitting a 2025 record](/blog/vinyl-sales-record-2025) is one of the few places it's growing rather than concentrating.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What percentage of DJs on Resident Advisor are actually booking gigs?

According to Attack Magazine's analysis of Resident Advisor's own booking database, only 16 percent of all 134,147 DJ profiles (21,351 DJs) had at least one future booking listed as of May 26, 2025. Just 1.6 percent, 2,175 DJs, had five or more upcoming shows, the frequency researchers used as a rough proxy for a plausible full-time touring calendar.

### How much does a DJ actually get paid per gig in the UK?

There is no verified public figure. We could not find a published DJ fee index, and an earlier version of this piece cited a GBP 100 to GBP 400 band that did not trace to its source. What can be said is that GigXchange's 2026 report puts typical UK musician income at GBP 20,700, with 43 percent of musicians earning under GBP 14,000, and session dates at GBP 180 to GBP 400 per session at Musicians' Union rates.

### Do most emerging electronic artists have day jobs?

Yes. Toolroom Academy's "From Mix to Mainstage" report, based on a survey of more than 250 emerging electronic artists, found that 82 percent rely on a job outside music to support themselves, 56 percent working it full-time and 26 percent part-time. The same survey found 76 percent of respondents don't believe electronic music is currently a financially sustainable career.

### Is there a public figure for what headline DJs are paid?

Not one we can verify. Reported numbers for individual DJs circulate in the press, but we found no reliable, sourced figure for any named artist and have removed the ones an earlier version carried. The most reliable thing in the public record is the pattern that fees at the top of the market are far above those in the middle, and that complaint is documented in our piece on DJ fees and the underground.

### Is this data based on RTS.FM's own DJs or listeners?

No. This piece synthesizes three independently published, external reports: Resident Advisor's own booking database as analyzed by Attack Magazine, GigXchange's UK earnings report, and Toolroom Academy's survey of emerging artists, researched with Audience Strategies. RTS.FM did not conduct its own survey for this article; every figure here is attributed to its original source in the text above.

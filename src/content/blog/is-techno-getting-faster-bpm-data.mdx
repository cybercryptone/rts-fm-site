"Techno is getting faster" is one of those claims that circulates so often in DJ booths and comment sections that nobody bothers to check it anymore. It gets repeated about the genre broadly and, more specifically and more loudly, about hard techno, the raw, distorted strain of the sound that RTS.FM covered earlier this year when we looked at [its American boom past 180 BPM](/blog/hard-techno-american-boom). That piece was careful to separate hardtekk and hardcore's tempo claims from hard techno proper. This one asks a narrower, more basic question: what does an actual, disclosed-methodology dataset say about median tempo over time, for techno as a whole and for hard techno specifically?

The answer, courtesy of tempo-analysis site Mixgraph, is not the clean upward line the popular narrative assumes. General techno, pooling every subgenre in a 43,989-track catalog, really has gotten measurably faster since 1991. Hard techno, the exact subgenre most associated with the "everything's speeding up" story, has not, at least not by this dataset. Both things are true at once, and treating them as if they must agree is exactly the kind of received wisdom worth being skeptical of.

## What does the general techno data actually show?

Mixgraph's [tempo analysis for techno](https://mixgraph.io/bpm-for/techno) is unusually transparent about its own limitations, which is part of why it's worth taking seriously. The site analyzed 43,989 techno tracks in total, with its year-by-year historical chart covering a narrower slice, 9,735 tracks spanning 1991 to 2026. Before publishing any figures, Mixgraph removed 2,971 tempo outliers using a Tukey IQR statistical fence, a standard method for stripping out extreme values that would otherwise skew a median. It also corrected 1,874 tracks that its detection had flagged as running at half their actual tempo, a common error in automated BPM detection. Even after that cleanup, 4,008 tracks, or 9.1% of the total, carry a tempo the site's own system flagged for manual review, and those were left in the numbers rather than quietly dropped.

With that method applied, the finding is straightforward: techno's median tempo climbed 10.0 BPM over the period, from 128 BPM to 138 BPM. The highest median in the dataset was recorded in 2025, at 140 BPM. The lowest was 2013, at 126 BPM, meaning the climb wasn't perfectly linear, but the overall direction across three and a half decades is real and disclosed, not a vibe. Mixgraph's page references a full year-by-year chart for the intervening years, but doesn't publish those individual annual figures as extractable text, so what's verifiable here are the two endpoints and the high and low points, not a granular curve.

## Does hard techno show the same trend?

This is where the story stops being tidy. Mixgraph's separate [hard techno analysis](https://mixgraph.io/bpm-for/hard-techno), pulled and measured on September 27, 2026, applies the identical cleaning method to 9,941 hard techno tracks spanning 2012 to 2026: 162 outliers removed via Tukey IQR, 764 half-tempo tracks corrected, and 1,789 tracks (18.0%, roughly double the flag rate of the general techno set) marked for manual review and still included.

The result runs directly against the acceleration narrative. Mixgraph describes hard techno's median as having "stayed remarkably stable" across the period, with the middle half of tracks sitting in a 136 to 155 BPM range throughout. More strikingly, the highest median recorded in the entire dataset wasn't recent. It was 2012, at 155 BPM. The lowest came in 2016, at 128 BPM. If you were expecting a chart that climbs steadily toward the present, this is closer to a chart that peaked near its starting point and has moved in cycles since, not a genre accelerating toward some faster future.

That's a genuinely uncomfortable finding for a scene that talks about itself, constantly, as getting harder and faster by the year. It doesn't mean hard techno isn't intense, louder, or more aggressive-sounding than it used to be. It means that whatever's driving that impression isn't showing up as a rising median BPM in a 9,941-track sample measured the same rigorous way as the general techno figures above.

## So where did the "145 to 155 BPM" claim come from?

Anyone who's spent time in hard techno forums or gear-blog roundups has probably seen a specific-sounding claim: that the subgenre's median tempo climbed from around 145 BPM in 2020 to 155 BPM by 2024. It's a plausible number, cited confidently, and it's worth explaining exactly why it doesn't hold up next to Mixgraph's disclosed figures.

Tracing that claim back to its source lands on the kind of SEO-style blog post that's become common in gear and DJ-culture coverage, the sort with no disclosed sample size, no cited dataset, and confidence-through-phrasing instead of evidence, lines like "my research shows" and "my findings are clear" doing the work that a methodology section should do. Strip away the confident language and what's actually offered is two vague reference points: "early 2010s: approximately 135 to 142 BPM" and "2024 to 2025: approximately 140 to 155 BPM," neither one a real measured statistic, both wide enough to mean almost anything.

Compare that to Mixgraph's approach: a stated sample size, a stated correction method for outliers and half-tempo detection errors, a stated flag rate for tracks it wasn't fully confident about, and endpoints anyone can go check for themselves. That's not a guarantee Mixgraph's numbers are the final word on hard techno's tempo history, no single-day snapshot from one catalog should be treated that way. But it's the difference between a claim you can audit and a claim you're just supposed to trust because it sounds specific.

## If tempo isn't climbing, why does hard techno feel like it's getting bigger?

Because scale and tempo are different claims, and conflating them is where a lot of the confusion starts. RTS.FM's own reporting on [hard techno's American boom](/blog/hard-techno-american-boom) found real, sourced growth: SoundCloud's 2026 Music Intelligence Report clocked US plays on hardtekk-tagged tracks up 75% over two years, and Luminate's midyear 2026 report put dance/electronic as the fastest-growing genre in US on-demand streaming, up 18.9% year over year, a trend also visible in the broader momentum behind [electronic music's growth as a US genre](/blog/electronic-music-fastest-growing-genre-us). Those are numbers about audience size, upload volume, and streaming share. None of them are numbers about median BPM within hard techno specifically, and that earlier piece was careful to note the tempo claims in the SoundCloud report applied to the hardtekk/hardstyle/hardcore continuum, a different, historically faster lineage than hard techno proper.

What's plausible, and what Mixgraph's data doesn't contradict, is that hard techno's audience and commercial visibility have genuinely grown, pulling a louder, more aggressive, more festival-visible fringe of the scene into wider view, without the actual median tempo of the tracks people are making climbing to match the hype. A scene can get bigger, louder, and more culturally visible while its core sound stays roughly where it's always sat. Those are three separate claims, and only the visibility one has strong recent evidence behind it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is techno actually getting faster?

For the genre as a whole, yes, measurably. Mixgraph's analysis of 43,989 techno tracks found the median tempo rose 10.0 BPM between 1991 and 2026, from 128 to 138 BPM, using a disclosed cleaning method that removed statistical outliers and corrected half-tempo detection errors. That's a real, gradual drift, not a dramatic spike.

### Is hard techno getting faster too?

Not according to the same dataset measured the same way. Mixgraph found hard techno's median tempo "stayed remarkably stable" from 2012 to 2026, sitting in a 136 to 155 BPM range, with its highest median recorded back in 2012 (155 BPM) and its lowest in 2016 (128 BPM). That directly complicates the idea of a steady upward tempo trajectory for the subgenre.

### Why does hard techno feel like it's speeding up if the median isn't rising?

Most likely because its audience, upload volume, and commercial visibility have genuinely grown, which RTS.FM covered separately in [hard techno's American boom past 180 BPM](/blog/hard-techno-american-boom), rather than because the typical track's tempo is climbing. A scene getting louder and more visible is a different claim than its median tempo increasing, and the two get conflated constantly.

### What's wrong with the claim that hard techno went from 145 to 155 BPM between 2020 and 2024?

It traces back to a source with no disclosed sample size, no cited dataset, and language like "my research shows" standing in for actual numbers, just two vague reference ranges. That's a meaningfully weaker basis than Mixgraph's disclosed 9,941-track analysis, which found no such steady climb.

### How reliable is Mixgraph's data?

More reliable than most tempo claims circulating online because it discloses its method: sample sizes, a Tukey IQR outlier fence, half-tempo correction counts, and the percentage of tracks flagged for manual review rather than quietly excluded. That said, it's still a single catalog measured on a single day, September 27, 2026, and its full year-by-year figures beyond the stated endpoints weren't published for direct inspection, so it should be read as a strong data point, not an unchallengeable final word.

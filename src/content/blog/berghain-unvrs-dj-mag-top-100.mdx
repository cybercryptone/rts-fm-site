DJ Mag crowned [UNVRS], a 10,000 capacity "hyperclub" on Ibiza, the world's number one club in its 2026 Top 100 Clubs poll, published in mid April 2026. It's the first venue in the poll's 21 year history to reach number one in its debut year, and the first to top the list within 12 months of opening. Berghain, long treated as techno's spiritual home, dropped five places to 21st, the only Berlin venue to make the list at all.

## What is UNVRS and why did it win DJ Mag's 2026 poll?

UNVRS opened on Ibiza on May 30, 2025, built on the site of the old Ku Club, which ran from 1979 to 1995 before becoming Privilege, once certified by Guinness World Records as the planet's largest nightclub. Privilege closed in 2018 after financial trouble, and UNVRS rose in its place with a comparable footprint: roughly 10,000 capacity across a complex of themed rooms, LED tunnels, art installations and a main floor built for arena scale production rather than a single dark room and a soundsystem.

The venue is run by The Night League, the promotions and hospitality group led by CEO Yann Pissenem, who also operates Ushuaïa and Hï Ibiza, both of which sit inside DJ Mag's 2026 top four alongside UNVRS. Pissenem has described the club as "a long term vision to explore new possibilities in music, technology and human connection," language that leans hard into the "hyperclub" tag his team coined for it, a hybrid of festival scale production and nightclub intimacy.

UNVRS's first season did the kind of programming that gets talked about industry wide: a b2b2b2b set pairing Carl Cox, Jamie Jones, Joseph Capriati and the Martinez Brothers, a Travis Scott walk on during an Anyma show, a Carl Cox set recorded for BBC Radio 1's Essential Mix, and a closing party with Anyma, Black Coffee, Peggy Gou and Rampa. That is a lineup built to travel on social media and generate the kind of vote turnout DJ Mag's poll rewards, as detailed in [DJ Mag's own report on the win](https://djmag.com/news/unvrs-voted-worlds-no1-club-dj-mag-top-100-clubs-poll-2026).

## How did Berghain fall from techno's summit to 21st place?

Berghain's story starts well before the building itself. In 1992, promoters Michael Teufele and Norbert Thormann began running Snax, a male only fetish party, out of a former air raid bunker in Mitte. That grew into Ostgut, a techno and queer nightlife venue that opened in 1998 in a disused train repair shed in Friedrichshain. When Ostgut's building faced demolition in 2003, the same team moved a few hundred metres to a decommissioned East German heating plant on the Wriezener Bahnhof site and opened Berghain in 2004.

The club built its reputation on a punishing sound system, a marathon booking policy that can run from Saturday night into Monday morning, and a door policy fronted for years by photographer Sven Marquardt that became internet folklore in its own right. Berghain topped DJ Mag's Top 100 Clubs poll in 2009, and in 2016 a German court classified it as a cultural institution, on par with concert halls and opera houses, qualifying it for a reduced tax rate. No other club carries the same symbolic weight in techno culture, which is exactly why its drop registered as news rather than a footnote.

A five place fall to 21st is not a collapse. Berghain remains the only Berlin club in the Top 100 at all, as [The Berliner reported](https://www.the-berliner.com/english-news-berlin/berghain-down-to-21-place/), and it still draws people from around the world specifically to queue for a door that might turn them away. But the symbolism is hard to ignore: a fan voted global poll now places Cologne's Bootshaus, at number 11, ahead of it, while three of the top four spots belong to large scale Ibiza venues built by the same ownership group.

## How does DJ Mag's Top 100 Clubs poll actually work?

DJ Mag launched the Top 100 Clubs poll in 2006, sister ranking to its older Top 100 DJs poll, and both operate on open, fan driven online voting rather than expert panels or ticket sales data. Anyone can vote once the poll opens each year, and DJ Mag has said the 2026 edition pulled in record participation, with voters from nearly every country in the world and 19 new venues entering the list, according to [DJ Mag's own feature on the 2026 results](https://djmag.com/features/dj-mag-top-100-clubs-2026-record-breaking-numbers-vote-our-annual-poll-of-worlds-best).

That open format has drawn criticism for as long as the poll has existed. Because anyone can vote and clubs can actively campaign for turnout, results tend to favour venues with the marketing budget, resident DJ rosters and social reach to mobilise large numbers of first time or one time voters, rather than venues judged purely on programming or sound quality. DJ Mag says it now runs an algorithmic screening process built from years of catching manipulation attempts, plus manual review of accounts showing unusual voting patterns, though it does not publish the details of how that system works. The upshot is a ranking that measures reach and campaign strength as much as it measures any critical consensus about where the best nights actually happen, which is worth keeping in mind whenever a venue jumps straight to number one in its first eligible year.

## Is Berlin's club scene actually in decline?

The Berghain headline landed inside a rougher year for Berlin nightlife generally. Clubcommission Berlin, the city's club and culture advocacy body, recorded 28 club closures against only 7 openings in 2025, a net loss of 21 venues and the worst year for the scene since 2009. Watergate shut its doors after its final New Year's Eve party going into 2025, Wilde Renate closed at the end of 2025, and SchwuZ, the city's oldest queer club, filed for bankruptcy in August 2025. About Blank and Loophole are among the other names that have gone dark in recent years.

Rising commercial rents are the most cited cause: reports point to rent increases of roughly 38 to 60 percent in central districts over five years, alongside higher staffing and energy costs, as former industrial buildings that once housed clubs get redeveloped into housing and offices. There are counterexamples, including AMT, which opened in early 2026 under the S-Bahn arches near Alexanderplatz with a 1,000 capacity room and a custom sound system, but the overall trend has been one of contraction rather than growth. Against that backdrop, Berghain holding a Top 100 spot at all looks less like a failure and more like an outlier of stability.

## Why does the hyperclub versus underground debate matter now?

The "hyperclub" label is deliberate positioning by The Night League, meant to describe something between a festival main stage and a nightclub, and it has not landed well with everyone who follows dance music culture closely. Reaction to UNVRS since its opening has included plenty of skepticism from ravers questioning whether a 10,000 capacity, multi room complex with luxury branding can deliver on the values that built Ibiza's and Berlin's reputations in the first place: cheap entry, DJ led programming, and a crowd there for the music rather than the production budget, concerns raised in coverage such as [MusicTech's report on the club's reception](https://musictech.com/news/events/ibiza-unvrs-luxury-hyperclub-alienates-dance-fans/).

That tension is not new. Ibiza has cycled through eras of scrappy DIY parties and glossy superclub spectacle since the 1980s, and Berlin's own techno scene has always sat in some friction with the city's tourism economy, which markets Berghain's mystique while pricing out the smaller rooms around it. What DJ Mag's 2026 poll makes concrete is that the commercial, high production model is currently winning the popularity contest, at least among whoever shows up to vote, while the club most associated with underground credibility slides in the same ranking. Whether that reflects a real shift in where the best nights are happening, or simply reflects which venues have the marketing muscle to turn out votes, is the argument playing out across DJ forums, Berlin's own trade press and international outlets covering the story.
